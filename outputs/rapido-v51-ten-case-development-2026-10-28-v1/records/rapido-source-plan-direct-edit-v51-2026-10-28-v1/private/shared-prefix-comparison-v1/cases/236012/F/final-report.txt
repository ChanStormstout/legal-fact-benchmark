The legal dispute centers on the termination of a permanent leasehold interest in survey No. 86/2 at Mundhava, Poona district, and the procedural requirements for enforcing forfeiture due to non-payment of rent. The principal questions were whether notice under Section 111(g) of the Transfer of Property Act (TPA) is necessary for determining a lease for non-payment of rent where such lease was executed before the TPA came into force, and whether the High Court should have interfered with the discretion of the lower courts in refusing relief against forfeiture [S71cadf6075e5].

**Historical Context and Lease Terms**
The respondents are heirs of Vinayakbhat, who claimed rights through his adoptive mother Ramabai. In 1863, Ramabai granted a permanent lease of two inam lands, numbered Pratibhandi Nos. 71 and 72, to Ladha Ibrahim Sheth. Present survey Nos. 86/1 and 86/2 together correspond to old Pratibhandi No. 71 [S7d318e964c98]. The lease included a premium of Rs. 999 and an annual rent of Rs. 80 during Ramabai’s lifetime, with subsequent payments equal to land assessment to her heirs. Crucially, the lease contained a clause stating that in default of rent payment, the tenant’s rights would come to an end [S7d318e964c98][S3417d2a4167c]. In 1870, Ladha Ibrahim sold these tenancy rights to Girdhari Balaram Lodhi. The sale deed reiterated that upon default of rent, the purchaser would have no rights over the property. A rent note was also executed, specifying payment in the month of Poush and confirming that default resulted in the loss of all rights [S80566af1f5f0]. The present defendants are grandsons of Girdhari Balaram.

**Litigation History and Waivers**
The record details a pattern of arrears and litigation spanning decades. In 1913, Vinayakbhat sued for possession based on forfeiture after six years of arrears. The court negated the defense that notice was required but granted relief against forfeiture [S906d8f1c474f][Sb7af28402465]. In 1928, another suit was filed for two years' arrears; however, the plaintiff waived forfeiture by accepting three years' rent and costs [S3516027004fb]. In 1931, arrears were paid via money order, which the landlord accepted, effectively waiving forfeiture [S023f57fe0bc0]. In 1934, government acquisition proceedings began for old survey No. 72. The landlord initially claimed full compensation arguing forfeiture had occurred, but subsequently accepted arrears sent by the defendant’s pleader, again waiving forfeiture. This allowed the defendants to receive Rs. 32,000 as compensation for their tenancy rights, while the landlord received Rs. 1,400 for his reversionary interest [S33d0851b01b8][S7e60a543f230][Sc326c5d7aa40]. In 1938, a suit regarding four years' arrears resulted in a finding of forfeiture, but relief against forfeiture was granted again. The District Judge noted that having obtained relief thrice previously, the defendants should not expect it a fourth time if they defaulted again [S25fbedabdec6][Se97eddf17deb][Sf043641ff118].

**The Present Suit and Competing Positions**
The immediate cause of action arose from a default on January 28, 1941. Vinayakbhat sued for possession of survey Nos. 86/1 and 86/2 and arrears of rent. The plaintiff alleged the rent was demanded but unpaid. Defendant No. 1 raised several defenses: (1) partition rights meant survey No. 86/2 fell to his share; (2) the rent note required the plaintiff to approach the defendants for payment, not vice versa; (3) no demand was made, so no forfeiture occurred; (4) the plaintiff fraudulently refused tender; and (5) statutory notice was required to enforce forfeiture. Alternatively, he sought relief against forfeiture [S3090e474a432][Sfed663771bc7][Saa2df4bbd6db].

**Procedural Findings and Legal Grounds**
The Trial Court decreed the suit for possession, imposing a condition allowing Defendant No. 1 to remain in possession of structures on survey No. 86/2 until March 1950. On the issue of notice, the Trial Court held that the lease terminated under Section 111(b) of the TPA (efflux of time/happening of event) rather than Section 111(g) (forfeiture), characterizing the default clause as one of nullity. Consequently, it ruled no notice under Section 111(g) was necessary [S5afdea9718d2]. Regarding relief against forfeiture, the Trial Court denied it, citing the defendants' 'contumacious conduct,' chronic non-payment over twenty years, and false defenses raised in previous suits [S006dde7cf872][S711fe9f5bf05].

Defendant No. 1 alone appealed to the District Judge, who confirmed the decree with modifications allowing the removal of buildings and trees within three months. The District Judge found the lease ended under Section 111(g) (forfeiture) but held no notice was necessary as the lease predated the TPA. It saw no valid reason to interfere with the trial judge's finding on relief against forfeiture [S6bd3b3405860][S6edc73b737af][S9e76c78f8751].

The High Court dismissed the appeal. It held that the notice provision in Section 111(g) was not based upon any principle of justice, equity and good conscience. Regarding relief against forfeiture, it held the matter was one of discretion exercised against the appellant by lower courts, and unless satisfied that discretion was not judicially exercised, it would not interfere; it noted non-payment seemed chronic and was not a case for equitable jurisdiction [S718056c333a4][Sb9a6de106bd4][S2dfb023a47c0][Sc5fd4abc4b7b].

**Unresolved Legal Grounds and Distinctions**
The Supreme Court appeal focuses on survey No. 86/2. The decisive legal conflict lies in the interpretation of the lease's termination mechanism. The Trial Court relied on Section 111(b), treating the default clause as causing automatic determination (nullity). The District and High Courts relied on Section 111(g), treating it as forfeiture, but exempted it from the notice requirement due to the lease's pre-1882 origin. The distinction between a 'clause of nullity' and a 'clause of forfeiture' is critical, as Section 111(g) explicitly requires written notice of intention to determine the lease, whereas Section 111(b) does not. The High Court's reliance on the temporal application of the TPA raises questions about whether statutory protections like notice apply to contracts formed under common law or local custom prior to the statute's enactment. Additionally, the repeated waivers of forfeiture in 1928, 1931, and 1934 complicate the current claim of absolute forfeiture, invoking principles of estoppel and waiver under Sections 112 and 115 of the Evidence Act, though the lower courts focused on the discretionary denial of relief under Section 114 of the TPA due to chronic default.
