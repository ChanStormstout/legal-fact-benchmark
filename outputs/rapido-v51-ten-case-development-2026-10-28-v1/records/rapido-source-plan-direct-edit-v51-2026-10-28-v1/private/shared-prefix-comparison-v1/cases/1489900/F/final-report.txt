The dispute concerns the ownership and partition rights of land in village Manota, Tehsil Ferozepore Jhirka, Gurgaon District, involving descendants of five family branches: Dalmir, Dilmor, Chhinga (brothers), and collaterals Amir Khan (represented by his son Alif Khan) and Sharif Khan [S4b90a25edb67]. The plaintiffs, descendants of Dalmir, sued defendants Nos. 1–16 (descendants of the other four branches) and pro-forma defendants Nos. 17–19 (other Dalmir descendants). They claimed full ownership of 819 Bighas 19 Biswas, asserting that the contesting defendants were barred from partition and entitled only to produce of 140 Bighas 19 Biswas without paying revenue [S00199c6b49d5].

The defense argued that revenue records showed all parties as owners of equal 1/5th shares, entitling them to partition, and denied that settlement conditions restricted this right after the settlement term expired [Sfce6b2ed4bf5][S03736d37f9fe]. The Assistant Collector, trying Suit No. 35 of 1940-41 as a Civil Court under Section 117 of the Punjab Land Revenue Act [L153a5ae834d1], decreed for the plaintiffs on July 29, 1942, limiting defendants to produce rights [S3dcb2f432439]. This decree was reversed by the Lahore High Court on November 10, 1944, in Civil Regular First Appeal No. 259 of 1942 [Se4468e3042fe]. The High Court held that the defendants were proprietors with an inherent right to partition. It ruled that the restrictive agreement dated September 28, 1861, incorporated into the Wajib-ul-arz, was binding only during the currency of the Settlements and ceased to have effect upon their expiry. Furthermore, the renewal of these terms in the 1938-39 Settlement was not binding because the contesting defendants did not agree to them [S4b2b3a6e0136]. The High Court relied on a prior Chief Court judgment dated June 15, 1893, which held that the prohibition of partition did not survive the expiry of the Settlement period [S1e2ef59ee816]. That judgment, based on defendant admissions, interpreted the 1877 Wajib-ul-arz to mean general division was prohibited while in force, allowing separation among Dalmir's sons but not other co-sharers [S644c4367bcb6][Sc2d688a625ac].

Historical context reveals that the property was originally granted in 1822 to Dalmir by Nawab Ahmad Bakhsh Khan Rais, though this grant was unwritten and lacked contemporaneous records [S205e9a446ea9]. Documentary evidence spans four thirty-year settlements: 1839-42, 1872-79, 1903-08, and 1938-39 [S25b51ad4a1f3]. The 1861 agreement established a zamindari tenure where Dalmir bore profit and loss for the entire village, while other biswadars enjoyed produce rights without revenue liability [S417aaf0285ac][Sa3f85bc956f4]. This arrangement was described as a system where few owners shared revenue responsibility while others were exempt [S98d75152f4ca]. The 1877 Wajib-ul-arz recorded joint possession since Sambat 1880, noting that collaterals like Amir Khan and Sharif Khan were entered as proprietors over time, maintaining joint ownership without partition [Sde2ad06bee15][S1c8953c93a98][Sb36d38285a62]. Paragraphs 5 and 7 confirmed the 'bilijmal' (joint) tenure, stating Dalmir's sons paid revenue for all shares due to relationship ties, and no single sharer could reclaim banjar land without consent [S75f388f02fc5][S42da4091ca29].

A critical tension exists between revenue entries and settlement clauses. Khewat and Khatauni papers recorded all five branches as possessing equal 1/5th shares [S9eb062f6a7de]. The Jamabandi of 1937-38 also showed all five branches in possession of equal shares [S47def472a3c1]. Conversely, the 1903-08 Wajib-ul-arz clause 3 stated that only Dalmir's descendants could partition, barring others due to non-rendition of accounts [Sd9a4ca069cbc]. Witness Mehrab, a plaintiff, admitted that defendants were shown as proprietors in the Jamabandi and that he raised no objection to this or to sales by co-sharers, suggesting potential estoppel issues under Evidence Act Section 115 [S7c5282f5f79b][L65ec615070d2].

In the current appeal (Civil Appeal No. 61 of 1951), appellant counsel Dr. Tek Chand contended that the appellants had acquired title by adverse possession over the defendants' share for more than 56 years [S869d8c8c61ea]. The procedural history indicates the Supreme Court's final disposition is withheld. Key unresolved matters include whether the 1893 Chief Court judgment operates as res judicata under CPC Section 11 [L193e14a39240] regarding the specific parties and issues, and how the Supreme Court reconciles the inherent right of partition for proprietors with the historical settlement-based restrictions. The legal effect of the 1938-39 settlement terms remains contested given the lack of agreement by contesting defendants, and the applicability of Transfer of Property Act provisions to agricultural leases is not explicitly resolved in the provided record [Ld4e6570d01b0].
