**Legal Report: Competing Claims to Devised Properties Following the Death of Dhuj Singh**

**1. Factual Background and Procedural History**
The dispute concerns five properties bequeathed by Raja Bisheshwar Bux Singh, a taluqdar of Oudh, via a will dated September 11, 1929 [S93c962ca54fe]. Raja Bisheshwar died on October 16, 1930, leaving two sons: the elder, Bajrang Bahadur (the Plaintiff/Appellant), and the younger, Dhuj Singh (the deceased devisee) [S9f8a531db7f0]. The Gangwat Estate is subject to the Oudh Estates Act (I of 1869) [Sf6e73fc7626a]. Upon Raja Bisheshwar's death, the estate vested in the Plaintiff as the eldest son under the Act, while Dhuj Singh received only the five specific properties mentioned in the will [S16c7302fbc90].

Dhuj Singh died in 1940 without issue, survived by his widow, Bakhtraj Kuer (the Defendant/Respondent) [S9f8a531db7f0]. A dispute arose regarding the succession to these five properties. Initially, the Plaintiff succeeded in having his name mutated in revenue records, but this was set aside by the appellate revenue authority, which directed mutation in favor of the Defendant [S1b0d3314e6d9]. Consequently, the Plaintiff filed Regular Suit No. 1 of 1941 in the Court of the Civil Judge, Bahraich, seeking a declaration of title [S18189f85fb34][S99f7413e03c9].

The trial court dismissed the suit, holding that Dhuj Singh had a life interest followed by similar life estates for his personal heirs, with the ultimate remainder reverting to the holder of the estate upon extinction of the line [Sba226bb61b31]. The Chief Court of Avadh affirmed this decision on appeal [S6fa8295e645b]. The Plaintiff subsequently appealed to the Supreme Court [S2c031f54c36e].

**2. Characterization of the Properties**
The record distinguishes between the legal nature of the devised properties:
*   **Taluqdari Properties:** Four out of the five properties are listed in "List A" and are undisputedly taluqdari properties [S99f7413e03c9].
*   **Non-Taluqdari Property:** The fifth property is listed in "List B" and is admittedly not taluqdari in character [S29fb4571d5c7].
*   **Abandoned Claim:** Two additional properties listed in "List C" were initially claimed but abandoned during trial; they are not part of the current controversy [S29fb4571d5c7][S77cd77b33003].

**3. Competing Legal Positions**

**A. The Plaintiff’s (Elder Brother) Position**
The Plaintiff advanced a two-fold argument:
1.  **Life Interest Interpretation:** He alleged that Dhuj Singh held only a life interest in the properties. Upon termination of this life interest (Dhuj Singh's death), the property should vest in the Plaintiff as the heir of the late Raja Bisheshwar [S77cd77b33003].
2.  **Statutory Succession (Oudh Estates Act):** In the alternative, even if Dhuj Singh had an absolute interest, the Plaintiff argued he was entitled to succeed to the *taluqdari* properties under Section 14(b) read with Section 22(5) of the Oudh Estates Act. This section allegedly prioritizes the brother over the widow in succession to taluqdari estates [S3e6f868167e9]. Counsel further argued that because Dhuj Singh fell within the category of persons enumerated in Clause (1) of Section 13-A of the Act, he held the properties subject to the same rules of succession applicable to the taluqdar himself, thereby attracting Section 22(5) [S587432b29f54][S1229b046ee9f][S08139e99b23b].

**B. The Defendant’s (Widow) Position**
The Defendant resisted the claim primarily on the ground of testamentary freedom. She argued that Raja Bisheshwar, as full owner, was competent to dispose of the properties as he liked. Under the terms of the will, she contended that the properties vested in her after Dhuj Singh's death. Her substantive construction of the will was that it created a life estate for Dhuj Singh, followed by a devise in favor of the widow as his "personal heir" [Sea2ab0080131].

**4. Analysis of the Will and Lower Court Findings**

The core issue hinges on the proper construction of the will [S94783f81455f]. The trial judge translated the document into paragraphs, which the Chief Court found useful [S0f4c27edeac3]. Key provisions include:
*   **Purpose:** To ensure the younger son and his heirs/successors "generation after generation" do not feel trouble or quarrel [S79bb88809623].
*   **Grant to Dhuj Singh:** Dhuj Singh is to remain in possession as an "absolute owner," but with the reservation that he has "no right of transfer" [S36554a9c0fa0].
*   **Succession Clause:** If Dhuj Singh predeceases the testator (or presumably dies without issue, though the text says "at the time of my death"), his son, male heir, or widow may remain in possession as "absolute owner" [S060f91fbf4ce].
*   **Restrictions:** While Dhuj Singh and his heirs lack power of transfer, they exercise all other rights of absolute ownership. The proprietor of the main estate cannot eject them [Sf45dbac59824].
*   **Reversion:** If Dhuj Singh or his heirs become "ever heirless," the villages revert to the estate rather than escheating to the Government [S5903ed9e2c13].

**Prior Judicial Findings:**
The Civil Judge concluded that Dhuj Singh got a life interest, but similar life estates were created in favor of his personal heirs in succession, with the ultimate remainder going to the holder of the estate when the line of personal heirs becomes extinct [Sba226bb61b31]. Based on this, the Defendant (widow) was held entitled to the properties so long as she was alive [S6fa8295e645b].

**5. Remaining Interpretive Limits and Unresolved Issues**

While the prior courts' findings are reviewable attributed facts, several interpretive limits remain relevant to assessing the competing claims:

1.  **Ambiguity of "Absolute Owner" vs. "No Right of Transfer":** The will grants "absolute ownership" but restricts alienation. The lower courts interpreted this as creating successive life interests. However, the term "absolute owner" typically implies a fee simple. The restriction on transfer might suggest a trust-like arrangement or a statutory limitation rather than a mere life estate. The record does not clarify if the "no right of transfer" clause was intended to defeat the statutory succession rules of the Oudh Estates Act or merely to keep the property within the family line.
2.  **Definition of "Personal Heir":** The Defendant argues the will devises to the "widow as his personal heir." The will text mentions "son or whoever may be his male heir or widow" [S060f91fbf4ce]. It is unclear if the widow qualifies as a "male heir" (she does not) or if the disjunctive "or" allows her independent claim. The lower court's finding that she holds a life estate suggests they viewed her as part of the "heirs and successors" chain, but the specific legal basis for her priority over the Plaintiff (brother) relies on the will's construction overriding statutory succession.
3.  **Application of Oudh Estates Act Sections 13-A, 14, and 22(5):** The Plaintiff’s statutory argument depends on whether Dhuj Singh, as a devisee of taluqdari property, falls under Section 13-A(1). If he does, Section 14 subjects him to the same succession rules as the taluqdar. Section 22(5) would then potentially prioritize the brother (Plaintiff) over the widow. The lower courts did not explicitly reject this statutory application in the provided excerpts but relied on the will's construction to grant the widow possession. The tension between testamentary disposition and statutory succession rules for taluqdari estates remains a critical unresolved point in the record. Specifically, it is not clear if the will's provision for the widow constitutes a valid "devise" under the Act or if the Act mandates descent to the brother regardless of the will's intent for non-taluqdari or taluqdari items.
4.  **Distinction Between Taluqdari and Non-Taluqdari Properties:** The Plaintiff’s statutory argument applies specifically to the four taluqdari properties [S3e6f868167e9]. The fifth property is non-taluqdari [S29fb4571d5c7]. The record does not indicate if the lower courts applied different reasoning to the non-taluqdari property. For the non-taluqdari item, general Hindu law or the Transfer of Property Act principles regarding wills would apply, where the widow’s claim as "personal heir" or beneficiary might be stronger absent statutory restrictions. The unified dismissal of the suit suggests the courts treated the will's construction as controlling for all five, but the legal basis for the non-taluqdari item differs from the taluqdari ones.

**Conclusion**
The available record supports the prior judicial finding that the will created successive interests, allowing the widow to hold the properties for her lifetime. However, the Plaintiff’s claim rests on a potential conflict between this testamentary construction and the mandatory succession rules of the Oudh Estates Act for taluqdari properties. The decisive factor remains whether the will effectively bypassed the statutory preference for the brother (Section 22(5)) by creating a valid devise to the widow, or if the statutory framework renders such a devise ineffective for the taluqdari portions. The interpretation of "absolute owner" with a restraint on alienation is central to determining if the widow’s interest is a life estate (as found by lower courts) or an absolute interest subject to reversion.
