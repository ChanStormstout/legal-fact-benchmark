The present property claim rests on a retrospective reconstruction of the appellate and procedural record concerning the family of Devarao Bapuji Deshpande. The decisive events begin with the death of Devarao in 1904, leaving him surviving his widow Sunderabai (Defendant 1) and Gangabai, the widow of his pre-deceased son Shankar. As the sole surviving coparcener's heirs, these two women were the only members of the family surviving at that time [S20246ada4849]. In February 1934, Gangabai adopted Devaji (the Plaintiff) as a son to her deceased husband Shankar. This adoption was disputed by Defendant 1 regarding its validity [Sf7dcb524f39e].

To resolve this dispute, the parties referred the matter to the sole arbitration of V. D. Belvi. The arbitrator communicated an opinion suggesting an amicable settlement, resulting in an award made in terms of a compromise [S9dd7a3b732a8]. The award explicitly declared that the Plaintiff's adoption was not valid [S9f17902f1d48], that Gangabai's right of adoption was lost from the beginning [S3989c965b576], and that the Plaintiff could never become entitled to the family property [Sf6f730f0b668]. However, for the purpose of maintaining peace and preventing waste of family property, Defendant 1 agreed to pay the Plaintiff Rs. 8,000 as consideration. If unpaid, the Plaintiff was authorized to recover this sum from the family property in Defendant 1's possession [S8a33124e1ea0][Sfd0ce58a6982]. Additionally, Gangabai's existing decree for maintenance against Defendant 1 was to continue permanently [S46ac0b7a4796].

This award was converted into a court decree in Suit No. 291 of 1937 before the First Class Subordinate Judge at Belgaum. Crucially, the Plaintiff admitted the terms of the compromise, the award, and the receipt of the Rs. 8,000, leading the Court to pass a consent decree accordingly on August 6, 1937 [Sdd3b6960e932]. Defendant 1 continued paying maintenance to Gangabai until December 1943 [S65068c4339b6].

The legal landscape shifted following the Privy Council decision in *Anant v. Shankar* (July 26, 1943). Advised by counsel that she could adopt again, Gangabai adopted the Plaintiff as a son to Shankar a second time on December 12, 1943 [S03b0cc76dee1]. Upon learning of this, Defendant 1 adopted her daughter's son, Jivaji (Defendant 2), as a son to her deceased husband Devarao. It is alleged that Defendant 1 ante-dated this deed to November 28, 1943, to precede the Plaintiff's second adoption [S36367218d137]. The Plaintiff filed Regular Original Suit No. 330 of 1944 claiming possession of family properties based on the December 12, 1943 adoption [Sf9938846f754]. Defendants 1 and 2 raised defenses of res judicata based on the 1937 decree and estoppel [S38e4fbc658ac][S1fe7eb26b370].

The Trial Court found that the suit was not barred by res judicata. It held the Plaintiff's second adoption (Dec 12, 1943) proved and valid, while Defendant 2's adoption was invalid because it was subsequent in date to the Plaintiff's valid adoption [S91731545b1c1][S3a43f85b5230]. However, the Trial Court held the Plaintiff was estopped from claiming immediate possession from Defendant 1, granting him only the right to possession after Defendant 1's death [S3a43f85b5230][S2433e45f0607]. The High Court confirmed this decree [S8f3fb996ddf5]. Before the Federal Court, the factual findings that the Plaintiff's second adoption was valid and prior to Defendant 2's adoption were uncontested [Sd07f365b8d4e]. The remaining points of law were whether the 1937 consent decree barred the current suit via res judicata or estoppel [Scb38f598e6f7].

Applying Section 11 of the Code of Civil Procedure, res judicata bars suits where the matter directly and substantially in issue has been finally decided between the same parties litigating under the same title [L193e14a39240]. The 1937 decree addressed the validity of the *first* adoption (1934) and the Plaintiff's status *at that time*. The current suit relies on a *new* cause of action: the second adoption in 1943. A consent decree generally operates as res judicata on matters actually compromised. However, the declaration that the Plaintiff "can never become entitled" may be interpreted as binding only within the context of the 1934 adoption dispute. Whether a new, independent act of adoption creates a fresh cause of action unaffected by the prior compromise is a key open legal issue.

Regarding estoppel under Section 115 of the Indian Evidence Act, the Plaintiff intentionally caused Defendant 1 to believe the first adoption was invalid and accepted Rs. 8,000 in reliance on that belief [L65ec615070d2]. Estoppel prevents a party from denying the truth of what they previously asserted. The critical question is whether the Plaintiff's admission of the invalidity of the *first* adoption estops him from asserting the validity of a *subsequent, distinct* adoption. Estoppel typically applies to the specific facts represented. If the 1943 adoption is a separate legal event, the estoppel arising from the 1937 compromise may not extend to bar claims based on the new status, unless the compromise explicitly waived all future rights regardless of new circumstances. The record shows the Plaintiff admitted the terms of the 1937 award, but the scope of that admission relative to a later, legally distinct adoption remains unresolved.
