Based on the supplied materials, here is an assessment of the competing positions regarding permanent tenancy and termination in Civil Appeal No. 154 of 1952 (*Vinayakbhat’s heirs v. Defendants*), distinguishing the procedural history, legal grounds, and evidentiary findings.

### 1. Competing Positions on Termination and Notice

The central legal dispute concerns whether a notice under Section 111(g) of the Transfer of Property Act (TPA) was required to determine the lease for non-payment of rent, given that the lease originated in 1863 (pre-TPA).

**Appellant’s Position (Defendant No. 1):**
*   **Notice Requirement:** The appellant argued that no forfeiture occurred because the plaintiff failed to send a statutory notice as required by law [Saa2df4bbd6db].
*   **Payment Mechanism:** The appellant contended that under the terms of the rent note, it was incumbent upon the landlord to approach the tenant for payment, not vice versa; thus, mere non-payment without demand did not constitute default [Sfed663771bc7, Saa2df4bbd6db].
*   **Relief Against Forfeiture:** The appellant sought relief against forfeiture, arguing equitable jurisdiction should apply despite previous defaults [Saa2df4bbd6db].

**Respondent’s/Lower Courts’ Position:**
*   **Clause of Nullity vs. Forfeiture:** The Trial Court held that the lease term ("in default... rights would come to an end") was a clause of *nullity*, causing automatic determination under Section 111(b) TPA, thereby rendering Section 111(g) notice unnecessary [S5afdea9718d2].
*   **Pre-Act Lease Exception:** The District Appellate Court disagreed with the "nullity" characterization, finding the lease determined under Section 111(g) (forfeiture). However, it held that because the lease was executed prior to the coming into force of the TPA (1882), the statutory notice requirement of Section 111(g) did not apply [S6edc73b737af].
*   **High Court View:** The High Court affirmed that the provision in Section 111(g) regarding notice was not based on principles of justice/equity applicable to this pre-act context, effectively supporting the lower courts' refusal to require notice [Sb9a6de106bd4, S718056c333a4].

### 3. Leased Property and Rent/Payment Evidence

**Property Details:**
*   **Original Grant:** Permanent lease of Pratibhandi Nos. 71 and 72 (now Survey Nos. 86/1 and 86/2) granted by Ramabai to Ladha Ibrahim Sheth on July 1, 1863 [S7d318e964c98].
*   **Transfer:** Ladha sold tenancy rights to Girdhari Balaram Lodhi in 1870. Defendant No. 1 is the grandson of Girdhari [S3417d2a4167c, S80566af1f5f0].
*   **Acquisition Impact:** In 1934, government acquisition proceedings started for old Survey No. 72. Defendants received Rs. 32,000 compensation for tenancy rights, while the landlord received Rs. 1,400 for his reversionary interest [S7e60a543f230, Sc326c5d7aa40].

**Rent and Payment History:**
*   **Rent Terms:** Yearly rent of Rs. 80 payable during Ramabai's lifetime; thereafter equal to land assessment. Default resulted in cessation of rights [S3417d2a4167c].
*   **Chronic Default:** The trial court found that "even this paltry rent had never been paid in time during the last twenty years" [S006dde7cf872].
*   **Waiver Instances:**
    *   1928: Waived by accepting three years' rent and costs [S3516027004fb].
    *   1931: Arrears sent by money order and accepted [S023f57fe0bc0].
    *   1934: Landlord agreed to accept arrears, waiving forfeiture [S7e60a543f230].
*   **Final Default:** On January 28, 1941, rent was unpaid despite demand. Plaintiff alleged fraudulent refusal to accept tendered rent; Defendant claimed plaintiff refused to approach them [S3090e474a432, Saa2df4bbd6db].

### 4. Historical Litigation and Unresolved Legal Grounds

**Discretionary Relief Against Forfeiture:**
*   **Trial Court:** Refused relief under Section 114 TPA due to "contumacious conduct," false defenses raised in every suit, and uniform default since Defendant No. 1 attained majority in 1922-23 [S006dde7cf872, S711fe9f5bf05].
*   **District Court:** Confirmed the refusal, seeing no valid reason to interfere with the trial judge's discretion [S9e76c78f8751].
*   **High Court:** Held that interference in second appeal requires proof that discretion was exercised without proper materials or judicially. It noted non-payment had become "chronic" and was not a case for equitable jurisdiction [S2dfb023a47c0, Sc5fd4abc4b7b].

**Unresolved/Contested Legal Grounds:**
*   **Section 111(b) vs. 111(g):** The Trial Court relied on Section 111(b) (efflux of time/happening of event) to bypass notice requirements. The Appellate Court corrected this to Section 111(g) (forfeiture) but maintained the outcome via the "pre-Act lease" exception [S5afdea9718d2, S6edc73b737af].
*   **Estoppel/Waiver:** While specific sections like ICA 20 (Mistake) or CPC 11 (Res Judicata) are provided in the law list, the judgment relies heavily on factual findings of waiver (acceptance of rent) and estoppel-like behavior (chronic default negating equitable relief) rather than strict statutory application of those specific codes in the final reasoning presented in the excerpts.
*   **Permanent Tenancy Rights:** The distinction between the tenant's right to compensation (Rs. 32,000) and the landlord's right (Rs. 1,400) highlights the complex nature of the "permanent" lease, which nonetheless contained express conditions for termination upon default [S7e60a543f230].
