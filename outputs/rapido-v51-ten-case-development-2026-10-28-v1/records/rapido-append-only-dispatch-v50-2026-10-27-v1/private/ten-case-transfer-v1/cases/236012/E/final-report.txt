The appellate record in Civil Appeal No. 154 of 1952 (decided February 27, 1953) concerns a dispute over permanent tenancy rights in survey No. 86/2 at Mundhava, Poona district [S7a87f7dac1cd]. The proceedings involve the respondents (daughter and grandsons of original plaintiff Vinayakbhat) and Defendant No. 1 (grandson of Seth Girdhari Balaram), whose grandfather Girdhari Balaram Lodhi acquired tenancy rights via sale from Ladha Ibrahim Sheth in 1870 [S7d318e964c98][S3417d2a4167c][S80566af1f5f0].

**Competing Positions on Termination and Notice**
The central legal question is whether notice under Section 111(g) of the Transfer of Property Act (TPA) is necessary to determine a lease for non-payment of rent when the lease was executed before the TPA came into force [S71cadf6075e5]. 
*   **Trial Court Position:** The trial court held that the clause in the 1870 rent note stating that tenant's rights would "come to an end" upon default was a clause of *nullity*, not merely forfeiture. Consequently, the lease determined under Section 111(b) (efflux of time/happening of event) rather than Section 111(g), rendering statutory notice unnecessary [S5afdea9718d2].
*   **District Appellate Court Position:** The lower appellate court disagreed with the characterization of nullity, finding the lease ended under Section 111(g) (forfeiture). However, it held that no notice was required because the lease predated the TPA's commencement [S6edc73b737af].
*   **High Court Position:** The High Court dismissed Defendant No. 1's second appeal [S721f1d767bf4]. It held that the notice provision in Section 111(g) was not based upon any principle of justice, equity and good conscience [S718056c333a4][Sb9a6de106bd4], while separately noting that the Chief Justice distinguished the present case from prior decisions [S397134992e08].

**Relief Against Forfeiture and Discretion**
Defendant No. 1 sought relief against forfeiture under Section 114 TPA. 
*   **Historical Context:** Relief against forfeiture had been granted in suits filed in 1913 and 1938, while forfeiture was waived by acceptance of rent in 1928. In the 1938 suit, the District Judge noted that having obtained relief thrice, the defendants should not expect it a fourth time if they defaulted again [S906d8f1c474f][S3516027004fb][Se97eddf17deb][Sf043641ff118].
*   **Current Proceedings:** The trial court refused relief, citing the defendants' "contumacious conduct," chronic non-payment over twenty years, and false defenses raised in prior litigation [S006dde7cf872][S711fe9f5bf05]. The High Court upheld this refusal, characterizing the matter as one of discretion which the lower courts exercised judicially based on proper materials, noting that non-payment had become "chronic" [S2dfb023a47c0][Sc5fd4abc4b7b].

**Procedural Status and Scope**
The Supreme Court appeal is confined strictly to survey No. 86/2 [S7a87f7dac1cd]. While the trial decree imposed possession conditions allowing Defendant No. 1 to remain in structures until March 1950, the appellate focus remains on the validity of termination without notice and the propriety of denying equitable relief [Sb0a49071e37f][S721f1d767bf4]. The record confirms that forfeiture was waived in 1928 and again in 1934 by acceptance of arrears [S3516027004fb][S7e60a543f230]. The courts treated the 1941 default as a fresh ground for forfeiture where notice requirements were deemed inapplicable due to the lease's vintage [S3090e474a432].
