The appellate record concerns two distinct batches of suits involving the same parties and subject matter, consolidated for hearing before the Supreme Court following the abolition of the Privy Council's jurisdiction. The central issue is whether the zamindar (defendant/appellant) is entitled to an equitable set-off against mesne profits claimed by the patnidar (plaintiff/respondent), specifically regarding payments made after the date of delivery of possession.

**1. Chronology and Procedural History of Batch One (Civil Appeals Nos. 68–74 of 1951)**
The first batch comprises seven suits filed in September 1904 by Maharaja Bahadur Singh against Raja Ranjit Singh Bahadur. The plaintiff sought a declaration of title to chowkidari chakran lands and mesne profits from 1899 until recovery of possession [S208f88e5e58a]. The trial courts decreed these suits in August 1905, but subsequent proceedings involved remands on limitation issues, dismissal, and eventual decreeing on second appeal, affirmed by the Privy Council [S830ce6ec7c1c]. The plaintiff obtained actual physical possession in August 1913 [S70e21aff1be8].

In November 1918, the plaintiff applied for ascertainment of mesne profits. The defendant resisted, arguing that deductions should be made for munafa (profits), chowkidari dues, and cesses paid by him [Sce40b4b270ac]. In June 1927, the defendant filed additional objections claiming an equitable set-off for payments made *subsequent* to the date of delivery of possession [S5646ec95254b].

The Trial Court (Munsif) ruled on December 18, 1937, allowing deductions up to the date of assessment but disallowing the equitable set-off for the subsequent period [S5b45c374137a]. On appeal, the District Judge reversed this decision on December 16, 1938, allowing the equitable set-off for the period since delivery of possession up to 1927-28, subject to the condition that the defendant’s dues be deducted from the plaintiff’s dues until wiped off [S79974ae0348d][S791d52f699e4]. The District Judge reasoned that the litigation was tortuous, making it unfair to bar the plea due to its late introduction, and that substantial justice required accounting for the entire period given the parties' continuous relationship [Sa484a4f5665d][Sc5c05b52b2f8].

The High Court at Calcutta, in February 1945, modified the District Judge’s decree, disallowing the claim for equitable set-off in its entirety for the subsequent period and restoring the Trial Court’s decree [Sf6e09ab6eed2]. The zamindar appealed to the Privy Council, which granted leave; these appeals were later transmitted to the Supreme Court [S4e4d57d9764f][S939beff23bd9].

**2. Chronology and Procedural History of Batch Two (Civil Appeals Nos. 75–92 of 1951)**
The second batch arises from 18 suits instituted in December 1909 by Ganpat Singh and Narpat Singh (predecessors of respondents) against the predecessor of the appellant. The allegations mirrored those in the first batch [S204257182fa4]. The Munsif decreed the suits in September 1910, granting khas possession on the condition that plaintiffs pay additional rent to the zamindar [Sed4c5d0f8b82]. The plaintiffs obtained possession on November 23, 1910 [Safdf29418a37].

Procedurally, the High Court initially set aside the condition of paying additional rent, but the Privy Council restored the right of the zamindar to have an additional rent fixed as a condition of possession, citing established authorities [S5e2e18f631bb][Sd84feca50cd7].

Regarding mesne profits, applications were filed in December 1922 for the years 1906–1910. Objections were raised in April 1923 seeking deduction of rent due to the defendant [S5c298f7dc4b8]. In May 1927, the zamindar claimed equitable set-off for amounts due for rent from 1910 onwards and for revenue/cesses paid subsequently [Sa34cd2105e19].

The Munsif, on December 18, 1937, allowed deductions only up to the date of delivery of possession, rejecting the equitable set-off for the subsequent period [Sa34cd2105e19][Sc4819a8e10e5]. The District Judge, on December 16, 1938, allowed the equitable set-off for the period subsequent to delivery of possession, directing that if the defendant’s dues exceeded the plaintiff’s, the suit for mesne profits would be dismissed [S35c88a4edcdc][Se89720073e4f]. The High Court restored the Trial Court’s decree, disallowing the set-off for the subsequent period [Se89720073e4f]. These appeals were also transmitted to the Supreme Court [Sf53aded1459b].

**3. Competing Positions and Legal Conditions**
The core dispute centers on the temporal scope of permissible deductions. Both batches involve claims for "equitable set-off" of:
(a) Munafa (rent/profits) payable to the zamindar.
(b) Chowkidari dues and cesses paid by the zamindar.

The Zamindar (Appellant) argues that because he remained liable for government revenue and cesses even after the patnidar took possession, he should be allowed to offset these costs against the mesne profits owed to the patnidar. The District Judges in both batches accepted this view, emphasizing "substantial justice" and the peculiar history of the litigation which delayed the raising of this specific plea [Sa484a4f5665d][Sc5c05b52b2f8]. They treated the claim as a cross-demand arising out of the same transaction [Sc5c05b52b2f8].

The Patnidar (Respondent) and the High Court rejected this extension. The High Court limited deductions to the period *before* or *up to* the date of delivery of possession. The legal implication drawn by the lower courts (Trial and High Court) is that once possession is delivered, the patnidar assumes the burden of revenue and cesses, or alternatively, the zamindar’s liability for these post-possession items does not create a valid ground for equitable set-off against mesne profits accrued during the patnidar's tenure.

**4. New Appellate Grounds**
During the pendency of the appeals in the Supreme Court, the appellant sought permission under Order XIX, Rule 4 of the Supreme Court Rules to urge additional grounds [S17a65736889a]. These grounds challenge the calculation of the munafa itself, rather than just the set-off mechanism:
1. That the munafa should not be calculated based on the principles laid down in *Radhacharan v. Maharaja Ranjit Singh* [S059cada2a54b].
2. That the munafa should have been assessed on a fair share of the profits from the land [S6c0a22a9bfbe].

These new grounds indicate a shift in strategy from merely defending the *right* to set-off to challenging the *quantum* of the debt being set off. The record notes that Raja Bhupendra Narayan Singh died during the High Court proceedings, and the present appellant was impleaded as his heir [S4e4d57d9764f].

**5. Assessment of the Record**
The available record establishes a clear procedural divergence between the District Judge and the High Court regarding the temporal limit of equitable set-off. The District Judge permitted set-off for the post-possession period (Batch 1: 1913–1927; Batch 2: 1910–1927), while the High Court restricted it to the pre-possession period. The Supreme Court is tasked with resolving this conflict. The introduction of new grounds regarding the valuation of munafa adds complexity, requiring the court to determine if these points are admissible and if they alter the basis of the set-off calculation. The record highlights that the pleas for set-off were raised late (1927) compared to the initial suits (1904/1909), a fact the District Judges excused based on the "tortuous" nature of the litigation, whereas the High Court's restoration of the trial decree suggests a stricter adherence to procedural timelines or substantive legal limits on set-off rights post-possession.
