Based on the provided materials, here is an assessment of the claimed equitable set-off against mesne profits, separating the two batches of suits, analyzing the periods before and after possession, competing judgments, and new appellate grounds.

### 1. Separation of the Two Batches of Suits

The litigation involves two distinct sets of appeals consolidated for judgment:

*   **First Batch (Civil Appeals Nos. 68 to 74 of 1951):**
    *   **Origin:** Seven suits filed in September 1904 by Maharaja Bahadur Singh against Raja Ranjit Singh Bahadur [S208f88e5e58a].
    *   **Subject:** Declaration of title to chowkidari chakran lands and mesne profits from 1899 till recovery of possession [S208f88e5e58a].
    *   **Possession Date:** The plaintiff obtained actual physical possession in August 1913 [S70e21aff1be8].
    *   **Set-Off Claim Period:** The zamindar claimed equitable set-off for payments made *subsequent* to the date of delivery of possession (i.e., post-August 1913) and for munafa payable after that date [S5646ec95254b].

*   **Second Batch (Civil Appeals Nos. 75 to 92 of 1951):**
    *   **Origin:** Eighteen suits instituted on December 22, 1909, by Ganpat Singh and Narpat Singh against the predecessor of the appellant [S204257182fa4].
    *   **Subject:** Declaration of title to resumed chowkidari chakran lands, khas possession, and mesne profits [S204257182fa4].
    *   **Possession Date:** Plaintiffs obtained delivery of possession on November 23, 1910 [Safdf29418a37].
    *   **Set-Off Claim Period:** The zamindar claimed deduction by way of equitable set-off for amounts due to him for rent from 1910 onwards and subsequent payments towards revenue and cesses [Sa34cd2105e19].

### 2. Competing Judgments and Findings on Equitable Set-Off

The core dispute concerns whether the zamindar (defendant/appellant) can offset his claims for munafa (rent), chowkidari dues, and cesses paid *after* the patnidar (plaintiff/respondent) took possession, against the mesne profits owed to the patnidar.

#### A. Trial Court Findings (Munsif/District Munsifs)
In both batches, the trial courts adopted a similar approach:
*   **First Batch:** On December 18, 1937, the trial court allowed deductions claimed by the zamindar up to the date of assessment but **disallowed** the amount claimed by way of equitable set-off for the subsequent period (post-possession) [S5b45c374137a].
*   **Second Batch:** On December 18, 1937, the Munsif decreed mesne profits allowing deductions only for amounts claimed by the defendant **up to the date of delivery of possession**. He held the appellant was **not entitled** to any equitable set-off for sums spent on revenue/cesses or munafa/profits for the period **subsequent** to delivery of possession [Sc4819a8e10e5].

#### B. District Judge’s Appellate Findings (Reversal)
The District Judge reversed the trial courts, allowing the equitable set-off for the post-possession period.
*   **Reasoning:**
    *   **Substantial Justice:** The judge argued that since plaintiffs had been in possession and enjoying rents since 1910 (in the second batch context, though the first batch possession was 1913, the logic applied broadly to the "peculiar" circumstances), they were not entitled to possess land unconditionally. The court should do "substantial justice" [S9055a322c00a].
    *   **Late Plea Justification:** The plea of equitable set-off was raised late (1927), but the judge held it was acceptable because the litigation was "tortuous" with conflicting decisions, making it unreasonable to anticipate such a plea at the beginning [Sa484a4f5665d].
    *   **Nature of Claim:** The set-off was viewed as a cross-demand arising out of the same transaction, connected in nature and circumstances [Sc5c05b52b2f8].
    *   **Time-Barred Debts:** The judge stated that for equitable set-off, questions of time-barred debts or unascertained sums do not arise; it can be used as a shield [S9d8c557826e7].
    *   **Determination:** The set-off was allowed for the period from delivery of possession up to 1927-28, carrying interest at 6% per annum [S791d52f699e4].

#### C. High Court’s Final Judgment (Restoration of Trial View)
The High Court of Calcutta modified the District Judge’s decree.
*   **Disposition:** It **disallowed** the claim for equitable set-off in its entirety for the subsequent period (post-possession) and **restored** the decree of the trial court [Sf6e09ab6eed2].
*   **Implication:** This means the High Court rejected the District Judge's reasoning regarding "substantial justice" overriding procedural timelines and the characterization of post-possession liabilities as valid offsets against pre-possession mesne profits.

### 3. Analysis of Periods Before and After Possession

*   **Before Possession:**
    *   In both batches, the trial courts allowed deductions for amounts claimed by the zamindar up to the date of delivery of possession [S5b45c374137a] [Sa34cd2105e19].
    *   These deductions likely included munafa and cesses accrued while the zamindar still held legal or constructive rights or during the period where the status quo was contested.

*   **After Possession:**
    *   **Claim:** The zamindar sought to offset munafa (rent) and cesses paid *after* the patnidar took physical possession.
    *   **Legal Conflict:** The patnidar argued that once possession was delivered, the relationship changed. The zamindar’s liability to pay government cesses/revenue might continue if he remained the registered owner or liable under statute, but the question was whether this could offset the mesne profits owed to the patnidar for the period of wrongful detention prior to possession.
    *   **District Judge’s View:** Allowed offset, treating the parties' character as unchanged and viewing the demand as a cross-claim from the same transaction [Sc5c05b52b2f8].
    *   **High Court’s View:** Disallowed offset. This suggests the High Court found no legal basis to treat post-possession liabilities as a valid equitable set-off against pre-possession mesne profits, possibly because the causes of action were distinct or the "equity" did not support merging them despite the tortuous litigation history.

### 4. New Appellate Grounds

Following the abolition of the Privy Council jurisdiction, the appeals were transmitted to the Supreme Court of India. The appellant (zamindar) sought to urge additional grounds under Order XIX, Rule 4 of the Supreme Court Rules [S17a65736889a]:

1.  **Calculation of Munafa:** That the munafa (rent) should not be calculated based on the principles laid down in *Radhacharan v. Maharaja Ranjit Singh* [S059cada2a54b].
2.  **Assessment Basis:** That the munafa should have been assessed on a "fair share of the profits from the land" rather than the method previously applied [S6c0a22a9bfbe].

These grounds indicate that even if the equitable set-off principle were accepted, the quantum of the set-off (the value of munafa) was disputed. However, since the High Court disallowed the set-off entirely for the post-possession period, these grounds would only become relevant if the Supreme Court were to reverse the High Court on the issue of admissibility of the set-off.

### Conclusion

The record shows a clear divergence in judicial opinion:
1.  **Trial Courts:** Rejected post-possession equitable set-off.
2.  **District Judge:** Accepted post-possession equitable set-off based on "substantial justice" and the peculiar/tortuous nature of the litigation.
3.  **High Court:** Rejected post-possession equitable set-off, restoring the trial court's position.

The Supreme Court appeal thus centers on whether the High Court erred in law by refusing to allow the equitable set-off for the period after possession, given the complex history and the argument that the demands arose from the same transaction. The new appellate grounds further challenge the valuation of the munafa component of the set-off.
