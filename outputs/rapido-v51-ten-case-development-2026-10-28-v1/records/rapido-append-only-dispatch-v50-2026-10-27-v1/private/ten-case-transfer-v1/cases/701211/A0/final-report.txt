Based on the provided appellate and procedural record, here is an assessment of the competing paths to specific performance and compensation, distinguishing contract formation, prior lease issues, term, waiver, and procedural objections.

### 1. Contract Formation and Validity
The central dispute concerns whether a binding contract for a lease existed between Kalyanpur Lime Works Ltd. (the Plaintiff/Lime Co.) and the State of Bihar (Defendant No. 1).

*   **Formation:** The trial court found that a legal, valid, and binding contract was formed. This was based on the Lime Co.’s application dated January 25, 1934 (Exhibit A), and the Board of Revenue’s approval/order dated March 25, 1934 (Annexure B), which offered a lease for 20 years on terms in Chapter V of the Bihar & Orissa Waste Lands & Mineral Concession Manual. The Lime Co. accepted this offer via letter dated April 15, 1934 (Annexure C) [S8e11b47725c3][S2c4036b2445a].
*   **High Court Reversal:** The High Court reversed the decree, holding that while a contract to grant a lease was made out, it was void under **Section 20 of the Indian Contract Act** due to a mutual mistake of fact regarding the Government’s title to the subject matter at the time of the agreement [S4da221281e65]. The High Court reasoned that both parties proceeded on the assumption that Defendant No. 1 was fully competent to lease the Murli Hill, despite the pending litigation with the Kuchwar Company [S75fed3609fe9].
*   **Procedural Objection to "Mistake" Issue:** The record indicates a procedural irregularity regarding the "mistake" finding. The trial court had originally framed an issue on mutual mistake but deleted it on November 29, 1950, upon the plaintiff's objection, substituting it with another issue. The counsel for the defendants explicitly stated they did not challenge the validity of the contract on grounds of mistake of fact or law, nor was there such an allegation in their written statements [Sa23514d45d95][Sc8d351d04916][S651702051afd].

### 2. Prior Lease and Title Issues
The validity of the contract is intertwined with the status of the prior lease held by the Kuchwar Lime and Stone Co.

*   **Prior Lease Status:** Defendant No. 1 had leased the Murli Hills to the Kuchwar Co. in 1928 for 20 years. The Kuchwar Co. purportedly assigned its rights to Subodh Gopal Bose in 1933 without permission, leading to forfeiture proceedings [S830e8b76ae83][S861302b02ec6].
*   **Forfeiture and Re-entry:** The Government forfeited the Kuchwar lease on March 27, 1934, and re-entered possession before offering the lease to the Lime Co. [Sa7d4a6c96918].
*   **Litigation Impact:** The Kuchwar Co. sued for declaration that the forfeiture was invalid. While the trial court dismissed this suit, the Patna High Court decreed it in favor of Kuchwar Co. on February 7, 1936, and the Privy Council affirmed this on November 19, 1937 [Sd6c529f926cc][Sc0a06713e0fd].
*   **Possession Gap:** The Lime Co. obtained possession in April 1934 but vacated in April 1936 due to contempt proceedings initiated by the Kuchwar Co. Although the contempt order was later set aside by the Privy Council in 1938, restitution was not achieved [S6e36386cb99b]. The Kuchwar Co. eventually surrendered possession when its original lease expired on March 31, 1948, allowing Defendant No. 1 to re-enter [S75fed3609fe9].

### 3. Term and Essential Conditions
*   **Duration:** Both the trial court and the High Court acknowledged that the agreed term was **20 years**, commencing from the date of execution (or April 1, 1934, as per the offer/acceptance correspondence) [S06cd204b2cf0][S2c4036b2445a].
*   **Royalty Terms:** The trial court found that essential terms, specifically the distribution of minimum royalty (Rs. 7,500 for upper portion, Rs. 2,500 for lower portion), were settled in a letter dated January 19, 1935 (Exhibit A12) [Sa9049bcb69cf].
*   **Defendant's Plea:** Defendant No. 1 argued that the proposal fell through during negotiations and that no concluded contract existed because essential terms were unsettled [S9d91cc92c6cb]. However, the trial court rejected this, finding the contract complete [Sbeb571c7196c].

### 4. Waiver, Estoppel, and Acquiescence
*   **Trial Court Findings:** The trial court explicitly rejected pleas of limitation, estoppel, acquiescence, and waiver raised by Defendant No. 1 [Sbeb571c7196c].
*   **Conduct as Evidence:** Under **Section 50 of the Evidence Act**, opinion expressed by conduct regarding relationships can be relevant [L6052a06ef1a9]. Similarly, **Section 115 of the Evidence Act** establishes estoppel where one party intentionally causes another to believe a thing to be true and act upon it [L65ec615070d2]. The Lime Co. relied on the Government's conduct in granting possession and accepting royalty distributions to argue that the contract was recognized and acted upon, thereby waiving any defects in form or title claims.
*   **Waiver of Forfeiture:** While **Section 112 of the Transfer of Property Act** discusses waiver of forfeiture by acceptance of rent [L625a28da96a6], the record focuses more on the waiver of *defects in contract formation* or *title challenges* through the Government's active participation in the leasing process (offering, accepting applications, granting possession).

### 5. Competing Paths: Specific Performance vs. Compensation
The plaintiffs sought specific performance primarily, with alternative relief for compensation.

#### Path A: Specific Performance
*   **Trial Court Decree:** Granted specific performance against Defendants Nos. 1 and 2 for the period commencing April 1, 1948, until possession was delivered [S97682527e61d].
*   **High Court Denial:** Denied specific performance on several grounds:
    1.  **Void Contract:** Due to mutual mistake of fact (Section 20 ICA) [S4da221281e65].
    2.  **Statutory Non-Compliance:** Void under Section 30 of the Government of India Act, 1915, due to non-compliance with conditions for executing assurances [S4da221281e65][L2010baaf0da5].
    3.  **Reconstruction of Agreement:** Granting relief would amount to reconstructing the agreement, which is impermissible under Section 18(a) of the Specific Relief Act [S4da221281e65].
    4.  **Readiness and Willingness:** The Lime Co. was deemed not ready and willing because it invoked Section 18(a) to seek relief beyond the strict terms of the contract [S06087d490791].
    5.  **Discretionary Bar:** The court declined to exercise discretion in favor of the Lime Co. given the circumstances [S06087d490791].

#### Path B: Compensation / Alternative Relief
*   **Trial Court Approach:** Decreed specific performance and left the amount of compensation for subsequent determination [S97682527e61d].
*   **High Court Approach on Section 15:** The High Court disallowed the claim for alternative relief under **Section 15 of the Specific Relief Act**. It held that the plaintiff had not relinquished all claims to further performance and all rights to compensation for deficiency or loss/damage sustained through default, as required by Section 15 [S952f65c826eb].
*   **Plaintiff's Attempt to Cure:** The plaintiff attempted to satisfy Section 15 requirements via an application dated February 18, 1952, claiming specific performance for the period after April 1, 1949, and expressly relinquishing claims for periods prior to April 1, 1948 [S2fb2a70d0810][S7093796118f1]. Oral arguments also clarified that no compensation was claimed for periods prior to the execution of leases [Sed355cb02b6e]. Despite this, the High Court maintained its dismissal.

### Conclusion
The record presents a conflict between the Trial Court's view that a valid, enforceable contract existed (supported by conduct, acceptance, and partial performance) and the High Court's view that the contract was void ab initio due to mutual mistake regarding title and statutory non-compliance. The path to **specific performance** was blocked by findings of voidness and lack of readiness/willingness. The path to **compensation** was blocked by procedural failures under Section 15 of the Specific Relief Act (failure to properly relinquish other claims) and the overarching finding that the underlying contract was unenforceable.
