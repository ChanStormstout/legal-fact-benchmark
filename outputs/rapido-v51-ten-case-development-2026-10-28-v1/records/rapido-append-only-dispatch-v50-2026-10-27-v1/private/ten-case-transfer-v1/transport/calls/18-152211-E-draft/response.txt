  "report": "The housing tenant challenge to the statutory exemption and unequal treatment is assessed through a retrospective reconstruction of the appellate record, separating the Board's status, the statutory scope, procedural history, and comparator evidence.\n\n**1. Proceedings and Procedural History**\nThe dispute originated in proceedings before the Court of Small Causes at Bombay, where the respondent (the Board) sought possession of premises occupied by the petitioner [S51643a81e6f4]. The trial court held that the Board was a local authority under Section 4 of the Bombay Rent Act and that this section did not violate Article 14 of the Constitution; it ordered delivery of possession but stayed the warrant until May 1952 [S808d8993dc70]. The petitioner moved the High Court of Judicature at Bombay in revision (Civil Revision Application No. 567 of 1952). The High Court found it difficult to classify the Board as a local authority but upheld the eviction based on Section 3-A of the Bombay Housing Board Act (introduced by the Amendment Act XI of 1951), which retrospectively extended the exemption to the Board. The High Court also rejected the Article 14 challenge [S69838ebf5310, S44341cce9445]. Leave to appeal to the Supreme Court was rejected by the High Court [S44341cce9445]. Consequently, the petitioner filed Original Jurisdiction Petition No. 271 of 1952 for special leave to appeal (No. 108 of 1952) and a substantive petition under Article 32 of the Constitution [Sd5970f2f108a, Sff6d93cc06d0]. These applications were heard together by the Supreme Court on December 18, 1953 [Sf0ec8991e3f5, S149698b5d452].\n\n**2. Board Status and Statutory Scope**\nThe factual matrix involves two distinct entities: the 'Bombay Provincial Housing Board' (constituted by Government Resolution in 1948) and the 'Bombay Housing Board' (established as a body corporate by the Bombay Housing Board Act, 1948) [S07a480fd64db]. The Sion Dharavi Camp, originally owned by the Government of India for military use, was purchased by the Government of Bombay in 1948 and entrusted to the Provincial Board [Sf0ec8991e3f5, S07a480fd64db]. On June 1, 1949, following a government declaration, the camp vested in the statutory Board [Sea1c62736f22].\n\nThe core legal conflict concerns the applicability of the *Bombay Rents, Hotel and Lodging House Rates Control Act, 1947* (the Rent Act). Section 4 of the Rent Act exempts premises belonging to the Government or a 'local authority' from its provisions [Se44560fe91e7]. The trial court relied on Section 3(3) of the Housing Board Act, which deems the Board a 'local authority' for specific purposes, to apply this exemption [S5f96a5787509, S808d8993dc70]. However, the High Court doubted this classification [S69838ebf5310]. To resolve this ambiguity, the legislature enacted Section 3-A via the Bombay Housing Board (Amendment) Act, 1951. This provision explicitly declared that the Rent Act shall not apply, nor be deemed to have ever applied, to land or buildings vesting in the Board, nor as against the Board regarding tenancies created by it [Sd74eb1d0ec62, S928d0b11125e, S1e41f8665839]. Thus, the statutory scope shifted from relying on the general definition of 'local authority' in the Rent Act to a specific, retrospective statutory exemption for the Board [S44341cce9445].\n\n**3. Comparator Evidence and Unequal Treatment (Article 14)**\nThe petitioner’s primary argument before the Supreme Court challenged the constitutionality of Section 3-A under Article 14 (Equal Protection of Laws) [Lb8acbb298b78, S92c014602292]. The petitioner contended that the exemption created an unreasonable classification. As comparator evidence, the petitioner pointed out that numerous Co-operative Housing Societies in Bombay, incorporated under the Co-operative Societies Act, are similarly situated with the same object of solving housing problems, yet their lands and buildings are *not* exempted from the operation of the Rent Act [S3a4cb5d57469].\n\nThe record shows the petitioner argued that the Board was not a local authority and thus could not claim the benefit of Section 4, and further that Section 4 itself was unconstitutional due to this inequality [Se9837a2f0396]. The High Court had previously dismissed the Article 14 challenge regarding Section 3-A [S44341cce9445]. The Supreme Court judgment notes that the only point urged by counsel was that Section 3-A offends the equal protection clause [S92c014602292].\n\n**4.
