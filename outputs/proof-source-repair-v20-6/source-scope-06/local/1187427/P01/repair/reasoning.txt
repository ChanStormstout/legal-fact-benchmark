The source is the Supreme Court judgment in B. N. Mutto & Anr vs T. K. Nandi, decided 29 November 1978 (1979 AIR 460, 1979 SCR (2) 409). Author: P.S. Kailasam J., Bench: Kailasam, Desai, Koshal.

Key facts from the judgment:
- Appellant landlord: Shri B. N. Muttoo (spelled "Mutto" in case title, "Muttoo" in body), Inspector General of Police.
- He leased property No. F-9, East of Kailash, New Delhi, to the respondent (tenant T. K. Nandi) from 15 September 1972 at monthly rent Rs. 2,200/- exclusive of electricity and water charges. Lease was for use of premises for residential and/or professional purposes only and not for commercial purposes. Lease renewed from time to time; respondent became monthly tenant under Delhi Rent Control Act, 1958.
- On 18 July 1974 landlord filed petition for eviction on grounds of misuser, subletting and bona fide requirement; registered as Suit No. 182 of 1974, still pending.
- First appellant B. N. Muttoo retired as Inspector General of Police on 30 November 1975. While in office he occupied premises No. C-II/77 Moti Bagh I, New Delhi, allotted by the Government.
- On 9 September 1975 Government took a decision that Government servants who own houses in the locality should vacate Government accommodation allotted to them within 3 months from 1 October 1975 (Office Memorandum No. 12031(1)/74-Pol.II dated 9 September 1975).
- On 9 December 1975 a notice was served on the first appellant by the Deputy Director (Admn.) stating the Government by its O.M. dated 9 September 1975 required all Government officials who own houses in Delhi and New Delhi and have been allotted Government residence to vacate before the stipulated date failing which penal rate of licence fee of market rate shall be charged besides necessary action to evict him from the Government residence.
- On the same day (9 December 1975) the appellant filed the present suit for eviction of the respondent (under section 14A(1)).
- On 16 January 1976 respondent applied for leave to defend. On 10 March 1976 the Rent Controller refused leave and decreed the suit.
- Revision petition to High Court; High Court allowed the revision and set aside the Rent Controller's order and remanded. Landlord appealed to Supreme Court (Civil Appeal No. 2302 of 1977), by special leave from judgment dated 6 December 1976 of Delhi High Court in Civil Revision No. 247 of 1976.
- Supreme Court allowed the appeal, holding that the landlord who retired before the date on which the notice to quit was given by the Government is also entitled to the benefits of section 14A.

Tenant's three objections: (i) landlord could not invoke s. 25B(5) because he was not a Government servant on the date of the petition; (ii) since the ground for eviction was the same as the pending earlier petition (O.S. No. 182 of 1974), the petition could not be entertained; (iii) premises were let for residential or professional purposes and therefore not let for residential purposes alone.

Rent Controller rejected all contentions, refused leave, decreed the suit.

High Court allowed the revision mainly on the ground that the landlord had retired from service on 30 November 1975 before the Ordinance came into force and was on that account liable to vacate the premises independently of his ownership of the premises in dispute.

Supreme Court holdings:
1(a) Section 14A does not require that the person in occupation of premises allotted by the Government should be a Government servant. Policy decision of 9 September 1975 related only to Government servants; but later the Government realized some provision should be made to get possession of premises let to persons other than Government servants who owned their own houses in Delhi. Although the circular dated 9 September 1975 and the notice served on the landlord support the view that the intention of the Government was to enable only those Government servants in occupation of Government accommodation and who owned houses to get immediate possession, s. 14A does not restrict the right to recover immediate possession to Government servants alone. The word "person" cannot be confined to Government servants because Government accommodation was provided not only to Government servants but to others as well (e.g., Members of Parliament and other non-officials). Nihal Chand v. Kalyan Chand Jain [1978] 2 SCR 183 at p. 190 referred to — there the Court observed there was "some force" in the view that s. 14A(1) was not intended for Government servants who had retired or been transferred outside Delhi, but did not decide the issue because on the facts the landlord was entitled to invoke s. 14A(1) notwithstanding retirement w.e.f. 30 November 1975. In Nihal Chand the notice was served on 30 September 1975, before retirement on 31 November 1975 (sic — the judgment says "31st November'" which is odd; likely 30 November). On the reasoning in that case the appellant here would not be entitled to relief; the question squarely arises whether a Government servant who retired before the notice was served is entitled to the benefit of s. 14A(1). The Court held: the word "person" is not confined to Government servants; the Court cannot help giving the plain and unambiguous meaning; retired Government servants as well as others in occupation of Government accommodation may become entitled to a special advantage, but the Court will not be justified in giving a meaning the words will not warrant. On this question the Court found itself unable to concur with the High Court's view.

1(b) It is not necessary in a petition for eviction under s. 14A to specify that the premises were let for residential purpose only. The words used in s. 14A are clearly different. Section 14A contemplates the owning by the landlord in Delhi of a residential accommodation. If he owns a residential accommodation he has the right to recover immediately possession of any premises let out by him. If the premises were one intended for residential accommodation it would not make any difference if the premises were let for residential as well as other purposes. Even if the residential accommodation was let for professional or commercial purposes, the premises would not cease to be for residential accommodation. Moreover the requirement in s. 14(1)(e) that the premises ought to have been let for residential purposes is not found in s. 14A(1). Busching Schmitz Private Ltd. v. P. T. Mengham & Anr. [1977] 3 SCR 312, referred to.

Also: It is admitted that the premises were let for residential as well as professional purposes. Section 14(1)(e) requires premises "let for use as a residence". It has been held that when premises are let for residential as well as commercial or for residential and professional purposes the provisions of s. 14(1)(e) will not apply. Dr. Gopal Dass Verma v. Dr. S. K. Bhardwaj [1962] 2 SCR 678 — construing s. 13(1)(e) of Delhi and Ajmer Rent Control Act 1952: premises let for residential purposes but used by tenant with landlord's consent incidentally for commercial/professional purposes cease to be premises let for residential purpose alone. On facts, substantial part used for professional purpose with consent. Kartar Singh v. Chaman Lal [1970] 1 SCR 9 — premises taken for residential-cum-business or professional purposes; rent deed; letter by landlord no objection to professional office along with residence; two rooms used as office; let for residence-cum-business purposes; licence plea rejected. The Court said it is not necessary to go into whether "let for residential purposes" would exclude premises let predominantly for residential purposes with a licence to use an insignificant part for professional purposes such as lawyer's or doctor's consulting room. The words in s. 14A are clearly different. It is common ground that the premises let were put up under the Delhi Development Authority's scheme for residential purposes. The only plea was that though put up for residential purposes it was let for residential as well as professional purposes. The requirement in s. 14(1)(e) that premises ought to have been let for residential purposes is not there in s. 14A(1). The Court agreed with the High Court that it is not necessary in a petition for eviction under s. 14A to satisfy that it was let for residential purposes only. This view taken in Busching Schmitz.

2. The submission that a previous application for possession pending would bar the s. 14A petition has no force because the grounds under s. 14A(1) are different and based on special rights conferred on the class of persons who occupied Government accommodation.

2(a) Scope of s. 25B is very restricted; leave to contest can only be given if facts would disentitle the landlord from obtaining an order for recovery of possession on the ground specified in s. 14A. Leave to contest under s. 14A(1) is not analogous to leave to defend under CPC Order XXXVII.

2(b) Sections 25B and 25C apply to both applications under s. 14(1)(e) and s. 14A. By introduction of s. 25C the condition imposed in s. 14(6) is varied — not applicable to persons who satisfy requirements under s. 14A. Section 25C(2) makes clear that not only under s. 14(1)(e) but also under s. 14A the term of six months prescribed in s. 14(7) is reduced to two months. By prescribing a specific period of two months under s. 25C(2) it is made clear that even an applicant under s. 14A would have to satisfy the conditions laid down by s. 25C, i.e., a period of two months should elapse before the landlord is entitled to obtain possession from the date of an order for recovery of possession.

Result: the landlord who retired before the date on which the notice to quit was given by the Government is also entitled to the benefits of section 14A; appeal allowed.
