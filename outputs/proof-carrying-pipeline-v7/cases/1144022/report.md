## Counterarguments

1. Procedural concession versus independent examination of the fire: Ramaswamy J. relies upon the company's express concession that the bank was dispossessed as alleged for the purposes of the suit and regards subsequent denial as impermissible (IK-1144022:L166-L168). Kasliwal J. instead examines the fire, relocation and bank's correspondence to conclude that possession had already been lost without force or unlawful conduct by the company (IK-1144022:L96-L106). The concession's precise procedural consequences remain central to the disagreement.

2. Voluntary vacation versus conditional delivery: The company contended that the bank voluntarily vacated following vis major and that a later refusal to redeliver possession was not statutory dispossession (IK-1144022:L145-L147). The contrary analysis emphasizes that the bank handed over access to facilitate repairs, retained belongings and maintained an intention to resume possession (IK-1144022:L137-L139, IK-1144022:L168). The distinction between loss of physical occupation and loss of legally relevant possession is decisive.

3. Force versus due course of law: Kasliwal J. finds the absence of force and other unlawful means sufficient in the particular circumstances to defeat the Section 6 suit (IK-1144022:L105-L109). Ramaswamy J. interprets the section as protecting against possession obtained through any method outside due course of law, including nonviolent, deceptive or ostensibly innocuous conduct (IK-1144022:L174-L189). Consequently, absence of physical violence does not dispose of the claim under Ramaswamy J.'s approach.

4. Licence expiry versus settled-possession protection: Kasliwal J. treats the expired licence and trespassory occupation as reasons why the bank cannot recover possession from the owner (IK-1144022:L90-L112). Ramaswamy J. acknowledges that post-expiry occupation may be trespassory but maintains that long, settled possession cannot be disturbed without due process (IK-1144022:L201-L210, IK-1144022:L240-L243). The essential opposition concerns the legal consequences of trespassory status, not merely its descriptive classification.

5. Ownership and admitted licence versus the summary remedy's independence from title: Kasliwal J. considers the admitted ownership and the bank's admitted licence status material to denying restoration (IK-1144022:L87-L90, IK-1144022:L110-L111). Ramaswamy J. emphasizes that Section 6 focuses on dispossession, consent, due process and timeliness, while leaving competing title claims to regular litigation (IK-1144022:L153-L164, IK-1144022:L175-L177). The High Court similarly reserved substantive rights while granting provisional restoration (IK-1144022:L83-L85).

6. Precedential distinction between tenants and licensees: Kasliwal J. differentiates cases protecting tenants holding over from cases involving expired licensees and relies upon the trespasser language of K.K. Verma and Lallu Yeshwant Singh (IK-1144022:L117-L124). Ramaswamy J. considers the precise question of an expired licensee under Section 6 unresolved by those tenant cases and draws upon authorities protecting established possession against unilateral recovery (IK-1144022:L223-L226, IK-1144022:L229-L240). The cited precedents must remain attributed to the opinions interpreting them.

7. Scope of D.H. Maniar: Kasliwal J. relies on its conclusion that a person remaining after termination of a licence has no continuing status as a licensee (IK-1144022:L125). Ramaswamy J. answers that D.H. Maniar concerned tenant-status protection under Section 15A of a particular rent-control statute and did not determine the availability of Section 6 restoration (IK-1144022:L203-L206). Its force as a controlling analogy is contested.

8. Settled possession versus transient trespass: Ramaswamy J. invokes authorities distinguishing effective, open and sustained possession from stray, intermittent or concealed trespass and treats more than fifteen years of occupation as settled possession (IK-1144022:L214-L221, IK-1144022:L240-L241). Kasliwal J. stresses expiry of the licence, refusal to renew, absence of acquiescence and subsequent loss of possession following the fire (IK-1144022:L90-L107). Neither approach should be reduced to an unconditional rule governing every trespasser.

9. Restoration despite eventual lack of title: Ramaswamy J. treats immediate protection against self-help as distinct from a later suit establishing ownership and entitlement to possession (IK-1144022:L175-L177, IK-1144022:L199-L200, IK-1144022:L240-L244). Kasliwal J. emphasizes that the company was the true owner and that separate title-based proceedings were available or already initiated (IK-1144022:L110-L116). These positions reflect competing conceptions of the purpose and practical operation of summary restoration.

10. Public-institution conduct versus adherence to the statutory remedy: Kasliwal J. regards the bank's prolonged occupation, public character and contribution to unnecessary litigation as important grounds for denying discretionary relief (IK-1144022:L112-L116). Ramaswamy J. acknowledges litigation abuse and delay but reasons that they cannot justify removal of a person in settled possession outside the prescribed process (IK-1144022:L131, IK-1144022:L242-L244). The High Court's criticism of the bank was expressly prima facie and not an adjudication of substantive rights (IK-1144022:L80-L85).

11. Renewal and classification remain distinct from summary restoration: The bank sought renewal and the company refused it (IK-1144022:L60-L61). Kasliwal J. treats the licence classification and expiration as admitted (IK-1144022:L89-L90). Ramaswamy J. notes the contractual renewal covenant and reserves the underlying lease-or-licence controversy for the regular civil suit (IK-1144022:L209-L210). Neither the bank's claimed renewal nor the ultimate substantive classification should be silently determined by the Section 6 reference.

12. Statutory appeal bar versus constitutional scrutiny: The bank argued that Section 6's bar on appeal and review should prevent Supreme Court interference under Article 136 (IK-1144022:L150). Ramaswamy J. expressly rejects that jurisdictional argument, holding Article 136 review plenary (IK-1144022:L252). This issue must be separated from his conclusion that the bank was entitled to restoration.

13. Opposing proposed dispositions and the actual Bench order: Kasliwal J. would allow the appeal and dismiss the Section 6 suit (IK-1144022:L126). Ramaswamy J. would dismiss the appeal and sustain the High Court decree (IK-1144022:L251-L253). The concluding order instead calls for placement before the Chief Justice of India to constitute a larger bench (IK-1144022:L257). The procedural referral identifies the unresolved disagreement; it supplies no substantive majority merits holding.
