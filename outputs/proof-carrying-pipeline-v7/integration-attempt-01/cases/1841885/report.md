## Counterarguments

F3 records that the trial court found no disclosed cause of action and an asserted statutory jurisdictional bar, rejected the plaint, and was affirmed by the first appellate court and High Court. These are genuine opposing lower-court assessments. They do not become target-court findings or establish the tenancy or possession merits. S4 addresses the narrower mixed-prayers rationale, not every conceivable defect in the plaint.

The respondent trust argued that Order VII Rule 11(a) and (d) applied because the plaint lacked a real cause of action and its remedies were barred under Sections 50, 51 and 80 of the Bombay Public Trusts Act. The judgment recognizes that illusory claims and omitted necessary material facts can justify rejection, but distinguishes the actual tenancy subject from the trust-affairs inquiry. The registry does not independently resolve every possible Section 80 objection.

The trust argued that the plaintiffs used ingenious drafting to introduce civil-court reliefs into a dispute governed by the special Act and also disputed the adequacy of the forcible-dispossession pleadings. Meaningful whole-plaint evaluation must consider such objections where relevant. Neither the procedural characterization in F5 nor S4 establishes that all material facts were adequately pleaded.

The trust's objection to prayer D has a legitimate limited statutory basis: a genuine Section 50 trust-affairs inquiry is different from a civil tenancy claim, and the judgment describes prior consent under Section 51 for suits of the specified kind. This supports S3 but does not transform that separate prayer into a bar against adjudicating tenancy issues.

F4 records competing asserted tenancy durations of eleven years and eleven months. They concern the same disputed tenancy relationship E8, and neither version is proved. The possibility that the tenancy had expired remains a substantive issue rather than a reason to treat the trust's preferred version as established at the Rule 11 screening stage.

F8 disputes the plaintiffs' alleged forcible eviction and asserts lawful possession-taking. F12 separately records trust counsel's claim that 22 of 44 tenants surrendered, while another passage describes surrender by most tenants. These descriptions remain unreconciled, and the alleged surrendering tenants are unidentified. Neither assertion establishes surrender by any particular plaintiff or defeats the opposing allegations as a matter of proved fact.

F13 records the trust's allegations of noncompliance with earlier High Court directions, arrears and improper litigation conduct. Such matters could be relevant to an appropriate trial determination or discretionary remedy, but they are attributed allegations, not proved defaults or established reasons for denying an injunction.

The judgment distinguishes Section 6 recovery following dispossession without consent and outside due process from the principle that an unlawful possessor cannot obtain an injunction against the true owner. That principle could oppose an injunction if its factual conditions were established, but the judgment does not find that these plaintiffs were unlawful possessors or complete the ownership and discretion assessments required in their particular case.

The trust additionally submitted that only the District Court, rather than the court in which the suit was filed, had jurisdiction. The Supreme Court expressly declined to decide that objection because it had not been specifically urged below. Civil adjudicability of the tenancy subject in S2 does not supply an adjudication of that separate exclusive-forum contention.
