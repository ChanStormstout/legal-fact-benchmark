## Counterarguments

F3 records the Wakf parties' competing ownership narrative, including historical religious acquisition associated with Peer Mahabari Khandayat, long-standing management by religious successors, alleged sanads, Wakf registration, official notifications, and a revenue enquiry. These were substantive challenges to government ownership, not merely an absence of opposition. F10 records the Supreme Court's finding that concrete supporting title evidence was lacking. That finding establishes evidentiary insufficiency in the judicial record, not the factual falsity of every asserted historical event.

The appellants expressly disputed the government's claimed acquisition, describing it as a concocted story in the arguments recorded at IK-1418721:L79. F7 nevertheless reports the Supreme Court's narrower characterization that the manner of acquisition was not under challenge. R1 must preserve that distinction: the stated absence of a challenge to the acquisition manner supports the court's treatment of Exhibit P1 as evidence but cannot erase the broader dispute over government ownership.

The asserted statutory acquisition basis does not resolve every question concerning the origin of government possession. The judgment distinguishes acquisition from assumption of guardianship under section 4 of the Ancient Monuments Preservation Act. F4 also reports the trial court's approximate acquisition chronology of 1900, whereas F1 identifies the claimant's reliance on the Act of 1904. Neither the precise historical transaction nor a reconciliation of these descriptions is independently established. Government management and monument protection cannot automatically substitute for ownership.

F5 records that the High Court affirmed the trial court's findings and additionally accepted adverse possession. This determination stands in opposition to the later Supreme Court assessment recorded in F18. The two determinations are not merged or classified as an unresolved factual conflict: they belong to different judicial stages, and the Supreme Court rejected the High Court's additional adverse-possession ground.

The government claimant's reported continuous possession for approximately one century, together with protected-monument status and construction expenditure, could support the affirmative evidentiary assessment but did not automatically establish adverse possession. Under the standard recorded in F15, possession must have the relevant hostile character against another true owner, with adequate publicity, continuity, exclusivity, animus, and duration. The specific deficiencies in F12, F13, and F14 therefore remain material notwithstanding evidence of long possession.

F1 and F2 disclose an apparent inconsistency between asserting ownership through original title and alternatively asserting acquisition of another person's title by adverse possession. The reported precedent requires consideration of renunciation. Nevertheless, F17 remains UNKNOWN: the supplied record does not independently establish whether, when, or how a legally effective renunciation occurred. S4 establishes the tension only. The alternative ground is not rejected merely by assuming renunciation never happened.

The Supreme Court's affirmative title finding in F11 and rejection of adverse possession in F18 are separate judicial conclusions. Neither can logically establish the other. The documentary evidence supporting original title requires its own assessment, while adverse possession requires its own pleading, proof, and legal evaluation. The proposed R6 conclusion is therefore limited to the independence of these grounds and cannot be expanded into an automatic determination of appellate relief.
