## Counterarguments

The plaintiff's strongest factual opposition was that the first defendant failed to dispossess him on 22 September 2000 and that forcible dispossession occurred only on 29 September. The High Court also found possession in the plaintiff on the date of suit. These positions directly opposed the Trial Court's acceptance of an earlier takeover, supported prima facie by Exhibits B-3 to B-6, B-8 and B-9. The Supreme Court identified the dispute and criticized the High Court's failure to confront the Trial Court's reasons; it did not independently settle actual possession. References: IK-74028:L73-L76, IK-74028:L87, IK-74028:L122-L123.

The plaintiff had a substantial contractual objection to termination: the High Court interpreted clauses 44, 45, 55 and 57 as requiring an opportunity to rectify relevant violations, considered principles of natural justice, and treated the termination as prima facie illegal. That position was not finally rejected on its substantive merits. The Supreme Court held that the need for rectification and the consequences of its omission belonged to the trial rather than detailed interlocutory determination. References: IK-74028:L84-L87, IK-74028:L119-L122.

The High Court's reliance on partnership dissolution developments was a potential response to the alleged unauthorized change in firm structure. However, the supplied judgment uses ambiguous language concerning cancellation of dissolutions, while the Supreme Court records a conceded change from a proprietary firm to a partnership and a contractual restriction requiring permission. The interaction of these facts and the ultimate contractual consequence remained unresolved. References: IK-74028:L85, IK-74028:L118-L121.

The plaintiff, through senior counsel R.F. Nariman, defended the High Court order as resting on relevant considerations and urged the Supreme Court not to interfere. The appellant, through senior counsel M.L. Verma, argued that the High Court failed to apply settled injunction criteria and failed to consider the Trial Court's reasons. The Supreme Court accepted the significance of the latter objections, particularly the omissions concerning possession evidence and compensability of injury. Counsel's submissions remain advocacy, not independent factual findings. References: IK-74028:L90-L91, IK-74028:L123-L124.

The High Court found serious prejudice and irreparable injury from termination without an opportunity to rectify. That finding supported interim protection but was insufficiently reasoned under the applicable equitable test because the High Court had not addressed whether damages would adequately compensate the plaintiff if he ultimately succeeded. The Supreme Court treated this omission as especially important. References: IK-74028:L80, IK-74028:L88, IK-74028:L100-L101, IK-74028:L124.

Restoration of allegedly wrongfully taken possession can be an available form of interlocutory mandatory relief under Dorab Cawasji Warden. Thus, a disputed takeover does not automatically rule out restoration. However, that authority requires a stronger case than the ordinary prima facie threshold, serious or irreparable injury ordinarily not compensable in money, and favorable balance of convenience. Its guidelines are not absolute, but the High Court had not adequately justified relief by engaging with those considerations and the contested evidence. References: IK-74028:L102-L111, IK-74028:L123-L125.

The Indian Oil Corporation v. Amritsar Gas Service precedent supported the distinction between invalid termination and entitlement to restoration, because the agreement in that case independently permitted termination on 30 days' notice and compensation was the appropriate remedy. But it did not establish that the present contract contained identical provisions or that the current plaintiff's damages would equal 30 days' earnings. Its relevance was to the remedial inquiry, not to a final calculation or determination of this plaintiff's contractual rights. References: IK-74028:L112-L116, IK-74028:L124-L125.

The Trial Court's view that a licensee could not obtain an injunction against the licensor was part of its reasoning, but the Supreme Court's analysis was more specifically grounded in the prima facie contractual position, the standards for interim mandatory relief, competing possession evidence, and monetary compensability. Restoring the Trial Court's order should not be treated as independently establishing an absolute rule that licensees can never receive equitable protection. References: IK-74028:L77, IK-74028:L82-L83, IK-74028:L92-L111, IK-74028:L117-L125.

The strongest reason for rejecting interim restoration without finally resolving the contractual dispute was the separation between provisional protection and adjudication of contested rights. Clause 45 and the conceded structural change gave the Corporation an arguable prima facie basis for action, while the alleged rectification requirement, legality of revocation, and possession remained contested. The High Court's order failed to respect those limits and to address essential interim-relief criteria. The reversal therefore did not logically require a conclusion that the Corporation would win at trial. References: IK-74028:L117-L127.
