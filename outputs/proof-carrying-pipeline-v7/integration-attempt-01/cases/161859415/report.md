## Counterarguments

The contesting defendants argued that the tenant alone could invoke Section 6 because the tenant, not the landlady, was physically dispossessed (IK-161859415:L80); this corresponds to the rejected physical-possession-only position of Veeraswami Mudali and Ramchandra (IK-161859415:L101, L103, L156).

The Madras ruling in (Kanneganti) Ramamanemma treated absence of the tenant as fatal to maintainability (IK-161859415:L104). The Supreme Court rejected that categorical rule while acknowledging that joinder may be desirable (IK-161859415:L157-L159).

The contesting defendants claimed joint-family ownership, co-ownership, and the seller’s lack of authority; defendant no. 4 separately alleged fraud and no consideration (IK-161859415:L74). These were title or validity disputes, whereas Section 6(1) permits possessory relief notwithstanding competing title and Section 6(4) preserves title litigation (IK-161859415:L84, L93).

An objection concerning the dispossessed tenant’s interests remains conceptually relevant: the Rajasthan report said it would be proper to join the tenant and allow a say in the relief (IK-161859415:L134-L136). The Supreme Court ruled only that non-joinder is not invariably fatal, not that the tenant’s subsisting interests can always be ignored (IK-161859415:L158-L159).

The statute bars suits brought later than six months after dispossession (IK-161859415:L88), but no initial filing date is provided; the 2001 trial-decree date cannot establish compliance or violation. The reported judgment does not separately resolve that possible procedural issue.
