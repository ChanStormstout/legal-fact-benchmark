Based on the supplied materials, here is an assessment of whether the permanent lease can bind the claimant mortgagee, distinguishing the relevant parties, timelines, findings, and legal questions.

### 1. Parties and Chronology
*   **Mortgagor:** Wazir Narain Singh (son of the original owner, Raja Nilkanth Narain Singh).
*   **Mortgagee (Original):** Chota Nagpur Banking Association Limited.
*   **Lessee/Defendants:** Hiraman Ram (Manager and Karta of the joint Hindu family) and his son Chohan Ram.
*   **Claimant (Appellant):** The plaintiff, who purchased the property at an auction sale in execution of a mortgage decree.
*   **Timeline of Events:**
    *   **1st August 1914:** Simple mortgage executed by Raja Nilkanth Narain Singh in favor of the Bank.
    *   **29th November 1921:** Bank obtains a mortgage decree against Wazir Narain Singh.
    *   **28th October 1922:** Bank purchases a third share of the property in execution of the decree.
    *   **5th November 1925:** **Crucial Date.** Wazir Narain Singh grants a **permanent lease** of four villages to Hiraman Ram and Chohan Ram.
    *   **14th August 1926:** Wazir Narain Singh executes a new mortgage in favor of the Court of Wards.
    *   **4th February 1929:** Plaintiff (via Court of Wards) files suit to enforce the new mortgage.
    *   **18th September 1931:** Final decree for sale passed.
    *   **6th April 1935:** Plaintiff purchases the property at auction.
    *   **16th November 1939:** Plaintiff files suit for possession against the lessees.

### 2. Challenged and Unchallenged Prior Findings
The appellate court (Privy Council) reviewed the findings of the Trial Court and the High Court of Judicature at Patna.

*   **Unchallenged Findings:**
    *   The plaintiff was **subrogated** to the position of the Bank (L131, L136).
    *   Defendant 2 (Chohan Ram) was **sufficiently represented** in the original mortgage suit (L131, L136).
    *   The High Court had considered the question of whether the lease rendered the mortgage security insufficient, calculating figures to conclude it did not (L134).
*   **Challenged/Withheld Findings:**
    *   The High Court had negated a contention regarding **constructive res judicata** (L132).
    *   The High Court had ruled that the lease was valid because it did not render the security insufficient (L134, L135).
    *   The Trial Court had held the decree was binding on the defendants (L131).
    *   **The Surviving Question:** The only issue remaining for the Privy Council was whether Wazir Narain Singh had the power to grant a permanent lease *prior* to the enactment of Section 65-A of the Transfer of Property Act (1929) so as to bind the mortgagee (L121, L136).

### 3. Open Legal Questions
The core legal question identified by the court is:
> "Whether prior to the enactment of Section 65-A of the Transfer of Property Act in 1929, a mortgagor in possession had the power to grant a permanent lease of the mortgaged property so as to bind the mortgagee." (L121)

This question hinges on the interpretation of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, specifically **Section 66** (Waste by mortgagor in possession), which was the governing law at the time of the lease (1925).

### 4. Assessment of Binding Effect
The assessment relies on the distinction between the statutory regime before and after 1929:

*   **The Statutory Framework (Pre-1929):**
    *   **Section 66 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (L4cb87a2c0ccd):** This section states a mortgagor in possession is not liable for allowing property to deteriorate but must not commit acts "destructive or permanently injurious thereto, **if the security is insufficient or will be rendered insufficient by such act**."
    *   **The Test:** The High Court applied Section 66, determining that the lease was valid only if it did *not* render the security insufficient. Since the calculation showed the security remained sufficient, the High Court held the lease valid (L134, L135).
    *   **Limitation:** Unlike Section 65-A (inserted in 1929), which explicitly grants power to lease subject to conditions (best rent, no premium, no renewal, max 3 years for buildings), the pre-1929 law did not explicitly grant a general power to lease. Instead, it restricted the mortgagor from acts that impaired security.

*   **The Nature of the Lease:**
    *   The lease in question was a **permanent lease** (L123).
    *   Under the pre-1929 interpretation of Section 66, a permanent lease could be considered "permanently injurious" if it impaired the security. However, the High Court found it did not impair the security based on financial calculations.

*   **Conclusion on Binding Effect:**
    *   **If the security is sufficient:** Based on the High Court's reasoning (which the Privy Council noted as a finding they did not challenge regarding the subrogation and representation, though the specific calculation was part of the High Court's reasoning on the lease's validity), the lease would **not** be considered "destructive or permanently injurious" under Section 66. Therefore, the mortgagor had the power to grant it, and it would **bind the mortgagee**.
    *   **If the security is insufficient:** Under Section 66, such a lease would be an act rendering security insufficient, which the mortgagor is prohibited from committing. In this scenario, the lease would likely **not bind the mortgagee**, and the mortgagee could claim the property free of the lease.
    *   **Distinction from Post-1929 Law:** The Privy Council noted the specific question was about the period *prior* to Section 65-A. Section 65-A (L502721c563fa) later codified the power to lease but imposed strict conditions (e.g., no premium, best rent, max duration). The pre-1929 law relied on the "security sufficiency" test of Section 66 rather than a statutory grant of power with specific lease terms.

**Summary:** The permanent lease can bind the claimant mortgagee **only if** the lease did not render the mortgage security insufficient at the time it was granted (November 1925). The High Court found the security was sufficient, thus validating the lease. The Privy Council's task was to confirm the legal principle that under the 1882 Act (Section 66), the validity of such a lease depended entirely on whether it impaired the security, rather than being automatically void or valid based on the mere fact of it being a "permanent" lease.
