Based on the supplied materials, here is an assessment of the objection to execution against the sons' partitioned property, analyzing the debt chronology, the compromise decree, and the procedural record, followed by the legal issues requiring resolution.

### 1. Chronology of Debt and Partition
The factual matrix establishes a clear sequence where the debt predates the partition, yet the legal characterization of the liability remains contested:
*   **Pre-Partition Debt:** On September 30, 1925, Baldev Das (father) executed a mortgage bond for Rs. 16,000 to secure a loan while acting as the manager of a joint Hindu family [S1b5d070c6f58].
*   **Partition:** On April 16, 1928, the sons filed a suit for partition, resulting in a final decree on July 20, 1928. The joint family properties were divided by metes and bounds, and separate possession was taken by the father and sons [S29c47135bf1b].
*   **Post-Partition Litigation:** On September 29, 1934, the creditor (Mst. Naraini) filed a suit for recovery. Initially, she claimed against both the mortgaged property and the joint family. However, upon the sons' application to be added as parties (arguing the properties were already partitioned to them), the plaintiff amended her plaint on February 7, 1935, to abandon the claim against the mortgaged property and seek only a money decree against Baldev Das personally [S764c24b293df; Sc4986463e975].
*   **Death and Compromise:** Baldev Das died on April 17, 1935. The sons and mother were brought on record as legal representatives. On November 20, 1935, a compromise was reached, resulting in a money decree against the "estate of Baldev Das" in the hands of his legal representatives [S9f014ca2f861; S3dc313ddd1ca].

### 2. Analysis of the Compromise Decree and Procedural Record
The core of the objection lies in the interpretation of the decree's wording versus the substantive law regarding ancestral debt.

**The Decree's Wording:**
The decree explicitly states it is passed "against the estate of Baldev Das deceased in possession of his legal representatives" [S71f0e4fd9183; S455c559dbee6]. The appellants argue that "estate" refers strictly to property Baldev Das owned at the time of his death, excluding properties the sons obtained via partition during his lifetime, in which he had no interest [Sd510ad69329d].

**The Procedural Record:**
*   **Waiver of Mortgage Pleas:** When the sons applied to be added as parties in 1934, the court recorded an order stating that since the plaintiff had abandoned the mortgage claim, the legal representatives could not raise pleas regarding the validity of the mortgage [S9f014ca2f861].
*   **No Formal Compromise Petition:** The record notes there was no formal petition of compromise filed and made part of the decree; the terms were based on statements by the parties' agents [Scd01843d342e].
*   **Lower Court Findings:** The Subordinate Judge found that the partition occurred in 1928 and the decree was obtained after partition. However, relying on Section 53 of the Civil Procedure Code, the Judge held that the sons' separate share was liable for the father's pre-partition debt (assuming it was not immoral) [S7861a3aa3085]. The High Court affirmed this, noting the appellants did not allege the debt was immoral [Sc8d43e93b852].

### 3. What Follows from the Record (Arguments Presented)
The appellants (sons) advance three primary contentions based on the record:
1.  **Strict Construction of Decree:** The phrase "estate of Baldev Das" limits liability to his personal assets at death, excluding the sons' partitioned shares [S5fb146ff05b; Sd510ad69329d].
2.  **Hindu Law on Ancestral Debt:** Even if the debt is valid, under Hindu law, the separate property obtained by sons on partition is not liable for the father's debt unless specific conditions are met. The appellants argue the decree, being post-partition, cannot attach their separate property [Sf9ce70b49f1a].
3.  **Procedural Impropriety:** Any obligation to pay the father's debt should be enforced only through a suit against the sons, not via execution of a decree obtained against the father alone, where the sons were added only as legal representatives after his death [S134e4fba6e50].

### 4. What Still Requires Legal Resolution
Despite the lower courts' findings, several critical legal questions remain unresolved based on the provided materials:

**A. Interpretation of "Estate" vs. Statutory Liability**
The primary legal conflict is whether the specific wording of the compromise decree ("estate of Baldev Das") overrides the statutory provision of **Section 53 of the Civil Procedure Code** [L4b2cec899b40].
*   *The Issue:* Section 53 deems property in the hands of a son liable for an ancestor's debt as "property of the deceased which has come to the hands of the son... as his legal representative."
*   *The Conflict:* Does the specific intent of the parties in the compromise (limiting liability to the father's estate) defeat the general statutory rule that makes partitioned ancestral property liable for pre-partition debts? The record shows the plaintiff voluntarily dropped the mortgage claim, but the compromise decree was still framed against the "estate." It is unclear if the court must interpret "estate" narrowly (as the appellants argue) or broadly (as the lower courts did, applying Section 53).

**B. The Effect of the Plaintiff's Amendment**
The record highlights that the plaintiff amended her plaint to drop the claim against the mortgaged property and sue only Baldev Das personally [Sc4986463e975].
*   *The Issue:* Does this amendment estop the plaintiff from later seeking execution against the sons' partitioned property? While **Section 47 of the CPC** allows the executing court to determine questions between parties and their representatives [L4b2cec899b40], the specific intent of the plaintiff to limit the suit to the father's person versus his "estate" (which might include ancestral shares) is ambiguous. The lack of a formal compromise petition [Scd01843d342e] makes the scope of the compromise open to interpretation.

**C. Validity of the Debt (Immorality)**
The lower courts dismissed objections because no plea of immorality was raised [Sc8d43e93b852].
*   *The Issue:* The appellants' written statement alleged the debt arose from "bad or speculative transactions" and was "illegal and immoral" [Sf7cdab5aa0ca]. Under Hindu law, sons are not liable for immoral debts. The appellate court must determine if the lower courts erred in not independently investigating the nature of the debt or if the burden of proving immorality lies strictly with the debtor in an execution proceeding.

**D. Procedural Status of the Sons**
The sons were added as legal representatives after the father's death [Sad53ad8225a2].
*   *The Issue:* The appellants argue that a decree against a father cannot be executed against sons' separate property without a specific suit against them. The resolution requires determining if the addition of sons as legal representatives in a suit against the father's estate automatically subjects their partitioned shares to execution under Section 53, or if the compromise decree's specific limitation ("estate of Baldev Das") creates a barrier to such execution.

**Conclusion**
The objection hinges on the tension between the **specific wording of the compromise decree** (limiting liability to the father's estate) and the **general statutory mandate of Section 53 of the CPC** (making partitioned ancestral property liable for pre-partition debts). The legal resolution depends on whether the court interprets the decree's reference to "estate" as a substantive limitation on liability or merely a description of the legal representatives holding the assets, thereby allowing Section 53 to apply despite the post-partition timing of the decree.
