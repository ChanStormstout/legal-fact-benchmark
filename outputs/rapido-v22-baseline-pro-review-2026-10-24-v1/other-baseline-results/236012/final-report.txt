### 1. Leased Property and Historical Litigation
The dispute concerns **Survey No. 86/2 at Mundhava in Poona district** (formerly Pratibhandi No. 71), which was the subject of a permanent lease executed on **1st July, 1863** by Ramabai to Ladha Ibrahim Sheth [S7d318e964c98]. The lease required a premium of Rs. 999 and an annual rent of Rs. 80 during Ramabai's lifetime, with rent payable to her heirs thereafter [S3417d2a4167c]. The lease contained a condition that rights would end in default of rent payment [S3417d2a4167c].

The history of litigation reveals a pattern of chronic non-payment and repeated waivers of forfeiture:
*   **1913:** Suit No. 99 was filed for possession due to six years' arrears. The court granted relief against forfeiture despite the lack of notice [Sb7af28402465].
*   **1928:** Suit No. 258 was filed for two years' arrears. The plaintiff waived forfeiture by accepting three years' rent and costs [S3516027004fb].
*   **1931:** Arrears for three years were paid via money order and accepted by the landlord [S023f57fe0bc0].
*   **1934:** During government acquisition proceedings, the landlord claimed forfeiture rights. The tenant offered arrears, which were accepted, waiving forfeiture again. The tenant received Rs. 32,000 compensation for tenancy rights, while the landlord received Rs. 1,400 [S7e60a543f230].
*   **1938:** Suit No. 982 was filed for four years' arrears. Forfeiture was held to have occurred, but relief was granted again [Se97eddf17deb].
*   **1941 (Present Suit):** Rent for four years remained unpaid. The plaintiff sued for possession and arrears. The defendant argued that no demand was made and no notice was given [Sfed663771bc7].

### 2. Competing Positions on Notice and Termination
The core legal conflict centers on whether a notice under **Section III(g) of the Transfer of Property Act** was necessary to terminate the lease for non-payment of rent, given that the lease was executed before the Act came into force (July 1, 1882) [S71cadf6075e5].

**Position A: The Trial and District Courts (No Notice Required)**
*   **Reasoning:** The trial court held that the clause in the rent note stating rights end upon non-payment was a "clause of nullity" rather than a clause of forfeiture. Consequently, the lease determined under **Section 111(b)** (conditional determination) rather than Section 111(g) (forfeiture). Since Section 111(b) does not require notice, no notice was needed [S5afdea9718d2].
*   **Alternative View:** The District Judge, while confirming the decree, found that the lease actually ended under **Section 111(g)** (forfeiture) but ruled that **no notice was necessary** because the lease was executed prior to the commencement of the Transfer of Property Act [S6edc73b737af].

**Position B: The High Court (Notice Not Required Due to Pre-Act Status)**
*   **Reasoning:** The High Court upheld the lower courts' view that no notice was required. It agreed that while the lease technically fell under Section 111(g), the statutory requirement for notice did not apply retroactively to leases executed before the Act's commencement [S6edc73b737af].
*   **Distinction:** The High Court noted that the Chief Justice in the judgment under appeal distinguished this case from a previous decision, implying a shift in reasoning regarding the necessity of notice [S397134992e08].

### 3. Relief Against Forfeiture and Discretion
The second major issue is whether the tenant should be granted relief against forfeiture under **Section 114 of the Transfer of Property Act** [L76b2d960cce4].

*   **Lower Courts' Stance:** Both the trial and District courts refused relief. The trial court cited the defendants' "contumacious conduct," noting that even the "paltry rent" had never been paid on time for twenty years. It emphasized that after the defendant attained majority (1922-23), he uniformly defaulted and raised false defenses in every suit [S006dde7cf872; S711fe9f5bf05].
*   **High Court's Stance:** The High Court affirmed that the matter was one of **discretion**. It held that unless the lower courts exercised their discretion unjudicially or without proper materials, interference in a second appeal was not warranted. The Court observed that the non-payment had become "chronic" and concluded that this was **not a case for the exercise of equitable jurisdiction** [S2dfb023a47c0; Sc5fd4abc4b7b].

### 4. Unresolved Legal Grounds and Procedural Issues
Several legal grounds remain contentious or unresolved based on the record:
*   **Retroactivity of Statutory Notice:** While the courts ruled no notice was needed due to the pre-1882 date of the lease, the underlying principle of whether Section III(g) applies to old leases remains a point of legal distinction, with the High Court noting a departure from earlier judicial precedents [S397134992e08].
*   **Waiver vs. Determination:** There is a tension between the plaintiff's repeated acceptance of rent (which under **Section 112** constitutes a waiver of forfeiture) [L625a28da96a6] and the plaintiff's insistence on forfeiture in the 1941 suit. The courts appear to have treated the 1941 default as a fresh breach where previous waivers did not preclude the right to sue, though the chronic nature of the default influenced the denial of equitable relief.
*   **Estoppel and Conduct:** The defendants' repeated failure to pay rent despite multiple opportunities and the raising of "totally false defences" in every suit [S711fe9f5bf05] serves as the primary ground for denying equitable relief, effectively overriding potential arguments based on estoppel or waiver.

In summary, the courts distinguished the two appeals by focusing on the **nature of the lease termination** (nullity vs. forfeiture) and the **applicability of notice requirements** based on the lease's age. Regarding relief, the courts uniformly denied it based on the **chronic nature of the default** and the **defendants' conduct**, viewing the situation as outside the scope of equitable intervention despite the statutory provisions for relief against forfeiture.
