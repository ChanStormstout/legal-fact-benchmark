### 1. Competing Claims: Ownership vs. Possession

**The Plaintiffs' Claim (Descendants of Dalmir):**
*   **Nature of Claim:** Full ownership and possession of the entire 819 Bighas 19 Biswas land.
*   **Legal Basis:** They argue that the agreement dated September 11, 1861 (incorporated in the *Wajib-ul-arz*), which restricted partition and granted them sole profit/loss liability, was binding. They contend that the defendants (descendants of Dilmor, Chhinga, Alif Khan, and Sharif Khan) are merely entitled to the produce of 140 Bighas 19 Biswas without paying revenue, effectively holding them as tenants or *Bhandadars* (village servants) rather than co-proprietors.
*   **Procedural History:** The Assistant Collector initially decreed this claim, holding the defendants had no interest in the remaining land. The High Court reversed this, finding the defendants were entitled to a 4/5th share as proprietors.

**The Defendants' Claim (Descendants of Dilmor, Chhinga, Alif Khan, Sharif Khan):**
*   **Nature of Claim:** Co-ownership (proprietorship) of the entire village, specifically a 4/5th share, with the right to claim partition.
*   **Legal Basis:** They rely on the *Wajib-ul-arz* entries which record them as owners of the remaining 4/5th share. They argue that the restriction on partition in the 1861 agreement ceased to be binding upon the expiry of the Settlement terms (specifically the 1938-39 Settlement). They assert that the right of partition is inherent in their right of ownership.
*   **Procedural History:** The High Court upheld their claim to a 4/5th share as proprietors, noting that the prohibition on partition did not survive the expiry of the Settlement period.

### 2. Analysis of Key Evidence and Findings

**The 1861 Agreement and *Wajib-ul-arz*:**
*   **Terms:** The agreement signed by Dalmir, Dilmor, Alif Khan, and Phusa (Chhinga) established a *Zamindari bilijmal* (joint zamindari) tenure. Dalmir was liable for the entire revenue, while the others were exempt from rent/Jama but liable for loss/profit on their specific cultivated areas.
*   **Partition Restriction:** Paragraph 2 of the *Wajib-ul-arz* stated that partition could only occur "of our accord" and that no single sharer could reclaim the Banjar area without consent.
*   **Judicial Interpretation:**
    *   **Chief Court (1893):** Held that the prohibition on partition did not survive the expiry of the Settlement period.
    *   **High Court (Current Appeal):** Reaffirmed that while the agreement was binding during the Settlement's currency, it ceased to operate as a bar to partition after the Settlements expired. The renewal of terms in the 1938-39 Settlement was not binding on the contesting defendants as they did not agree to it.

**Revenue Records (Khewat/Jamabandi):**
*   **Historical Records:** Early records (1863) show Dalmir as the sole owner/grantee, with others recorded as *Biswadars* (cultivators) who became owners of the produce without rent payment.
*   **Later Records:** The *Jamabandi* of 1937-38 (the last settlement before the suit) recorded all five branches as being in possession of **equal shares**. This contradicts the plaintiffs' claim that the defendants only hold a produce-right without ownership.
*   **Admission:** Plaintiff witness Mehrab admitted that defendants were shown as proprietors in the *Jamabandi* and that no objection was raised at the time.

**Prior Litigation (Res Judicata):**
*   **1891 Suit:** Alif Khan and Sharif Khan sued for a 1/5th share. The Chief Court decreed they were entitled to a 1/5th share **subject to the restrictions** in the *Wajib-ul-arz* while it was in force.
*   **Relevance:** This establishes that the parties previously litigated the extent of their shares and the validity of the partition restrictions. The current dispute centers on whether those restrictions survived the 1938-39 Settlement expiry.

**Scope Limits:**

**Unresolved Matters:**
1.  **Validity of the 1938-39 Settlement Renewal:** The High Court noted the renewal was not binding on the defendants. However, it remains unclear if the defendants' failure to agree was due to ignorance, waiver, or active objection, which could impact estoppel claims.
2.  **Adverse Possession:** Dr. Tek Chand contended that the plaintiffs acquired title by adverse possession over the defendants' share for over 56 years. The provided materials do not contain a final judicial determination on whether this period was continuous, open, and hostile enough to extinguish the defendants' proprietary rights.
3.  **Interpretation of "Equal Shares" in 1937-38 Jamabandi:** While the 1937-38 record shows equal shares, the plaintiffs argue the defendants only hold a "produce" right. The legal weight of the *Jamabandi* entry versus the underlying *Wajib-ul-arz* agreement regarding the nature of the defendants' interest (ownership vs. tenancy) appears to be the crux of the unresolved conflict.
4.  **Effect of Prior Litigation (1891):** While the 1891 decree established a 1/5th share subject to restrictions, the current suit seeks a declaration of full ownership. It is unresolved whether the 1891 decree operates as *res judicata* (CPC Section 11) to prevent the plaintiffs from now claiming full ownership, or if the change in legal interpretation regarding the expiry of the Settlement allows for a new claim.
5.  **Family Relationship and Estoppel:** Under the Indian Evidence Act (Section 50), family conduct can prove relationship. However, it is unclear if the plaintiffs' long-standing cultivation without objection constitutes an estoppel (Section 115) preventing them from now claiming full ownership against the defendants who have historically been recorded as co-proprietors.

**Conclusion:**
The primary conflict lies between the **historical restriction** on partition (1861 agreement) and the **statutory right to partition** upon the expiry of the Settlement term. The High Court has favored the defendants' right to partition and ownership of a 4/5th share, rejecting the plaintiffs' claim of sole ownership. However, the matter of **adverse possession** and the precise legal effect of the **1937-38 Jamabandi entry** versus the **1861 agreement** remain areas where the final appellate disposition (which is withheld) would likely provide the definitive resolution.
