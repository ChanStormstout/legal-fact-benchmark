Based on the supplied appellate and procedural record, here is an assessment of the competing claims to the five devised properties following the death of Dhuj Singh, along with the interpretive limits of the will and procedural findings.

### 1. Competing Claims to the Properties

The dispute centers on whether the five properties bequeathed by Raja Bisheshwar Bux Singh to his younger son, Dhuj Singh, vested in Dhuj Singh's widow (Bakhtraj Kuer) or reverted to his elder brother (Bajrang Bahadur Singh, the plaintiff) upon Dhuj Singh's death in 1940.

*   **The Widow's Claim (Defendant):** The defendant contends that the will created a life estate for Dhuj Singh, followed by a devise in her favor as his "personal heir." She argues that as the surviving spouse, she holds the right to the properties for her lifetime.
*   **The Elder Brother's Claim (Plaintiff):** The plaintiff argues that Dhuj Singh held only a life interest, meaning the properties vested in him as the heir of the late Raja upon Dhuj Singh's death. Alternatively, he argues that even if Dhuj Singh had an absolute interest, the *Oudh Estates Act* (specifically Section 14(b) read with Section 22(5)) mandates that taluqdari properties pass to the brother in preference to the widow.

### 2. Support from the Will and Procedural Record

**A. The Will's Language and Intent**
The trial court's translation of the will (L177–L189) provides the primary basis for the widow's claim:
*   **Life Interest vs. Absolute Ownership:** The will explicitly states Dhuj Singh is "simply entitled to maintenance" (L177) and shall remain in possession "as an absolute owner with the reservation that he will have no right of transfer" (L182).
*   **Succession Clause:** Crucially, Clause 4 of the will states: "If God forbid, Dhuj Singh may not be living at the time of my death, his son or whoever may be his male heir or **widow may remain in possession of the said villages on payment of the Government revenue as an absolute owner**" (L183).
*   **Ultimate Remainder:** The will provides that if Dhuj Singh or his heirs become "ever heirless," the villages will revert to the estate (L188).

**B. Procedural Findings (Trial and Chief Court)**
The lower courts (Civil Judge, Bahraich, and Chief Court of Avadh) ruled in favor of the widow:
*   **Interpretation of "Heirs":** The trial court concluded that the will created a life interest for Dhuj Singh, but also created similar life estates for his "personal heirs in succession" (L158).
*   **Definition of Personal Heir:** The court interpreted the phrase "his son or whoever may be his male heir or widow" in Clause 4 to mean that the widow is a designated successor who holds the property as an absolute owner for her lifetime, distinct from the general rule of reversion to the estate.
*   **Disposition:** Consequently, the plaintiff's suit was dismissed at both the trial and appellate levels, affirming the widow's title during her lifetime (L159).

### 3. Interpretive Limits and Legal Arguments

**A. The Plaintiff's Statutory Argument (Oudh Estates Act)**
The plaintiff's strongest argument relies on statutory succession rather than the will's specific wording regarding the widow.
*   **Section 14(b) & 22(5):** The plaintiff argues that because Dhuj Singh held the properties under a will from a Taluqdar, he falls under Section 13-A of the *Oudh Estates Act*. Therefore, the properties should be held subject to the same rules of succession as the Taluqdar himself. Under Section 22(5), the brother (plaintiff) would succeed in preference to the widow (L160–L162).
*   **Limitation:** This argument hinges on whether the will's specific grant of a "life estate" to the widow overrides the statutory default succession rules. The lower courts appear to have prioritized the specific intent of the will (granting the widow a life interest) over the statutory default of brotherly succession.

**B. Ambiguity in "Absolute Owner" Status**
The will creates a tension in its terminology:
*   It grants Dhuj Singh possession "as an absolute owner" but restricts transfer (L182).
*   It grants the widow possession "as an absolute owner" (L183).
*   **Limitation:** The procedural record does not clarify whether the widow's "absolute ownership" is limited strictly to her lifetime (a life estate) or if it includes the power to transfer or bequeath the property to her own heirs. The phrase "ever heirless" (L188) suggests a reversion to the estate if the line of personal heirs (including the widow) dies out, but the duration of the widow's specific interest remains the core interpretive limit.

**C. Procedural Status of the Appeal**
*   **Current State:** The current appeal (Civil Appeal No. 147 of 1951) challenges the Chief Court's affirmation of the trial court's decision.
*   **Withheld Disposition:** As per the scope, the target court's final reasoning and disposition are withheld. The record only establishes that the lower courts favored the widow based on the will's construction. The Supreme Court (or High Court) has not yet rendered a final judgment on whether the *Oudh Estates Act* statutory succession overrides the will's specific provision for the widow.

### Conclusion
The procedural record supports the **widow's claim** based on the explicit language of the will (L183) and the lower courts' interpretation that the will created a succession of life estates for personal heirs, including the widow. However, the **elder brother's claim** remains viable under the *Oudh Estates Act* if the court determines that the statutory rules of succession for Taluqdari properties (favoring the brother) supersede the specific life estate granted to the widow in the will. The final resolution depends on the appellate court's determination of whether the will's specific terms regarding the widow's "absolute ownership" are compatible with or subordinate to the statutory succession rules.
