Based on the retrospective reconstruction of the appellate record provided in the materials, here is the assessment of the claimed spousal inheritance priority against the daughter, distinguishing the relevant evidentiary and legal factors.

### 1. Marriage Evidence: Presumption vs. Rebuttal
The core dispute hinges on whether the appellant (plaintiff) successfully proved that Ram Piari was his legally wedded wife.

*   **Trial Court Finding:** The trial court accepted the plaintiff's claim, ruling that Ram Piari was his legally married wife based on direct evidence from four witnesses (a family priest and three villagers) and circumstantial evidence regarding cohabitation and social treatment.
*   **High Court Finding (Rebuttal):** The High Court reversed this, holding that the presumption of marriage arising from long cohabitation was "completely rebutted."
    *   **Direct Evidence Deficiencies:** The High Court found the four witnesses claiming to have officiated or witnessed the marriage "unconvincing." They were described as either hired witnesses or interested parties (L195, L215).
    *   **Circumstantial Evidence & Section 50:** The High Court applied **Section 50 of the Indian Evidence Act** (L6052a06ef1a9), which allows opinion on relationship expressed by conduct by persons with special means of knowledge. However, the High Court ruled that the witnesses did not strictly comply with this section because they lacked "special means of knowledge" regarding the specific relationship, and their testimony was merely opinion rather than conduct-based fact (L196, L218).
    *   **Contradictory Testimony:** The High Court noted strong contradictory evidence, including the deposition of Ram Piari's own mother (Ganga) stating Ram Piari was never married to the plaintiff, and the plaintiff's own daughter (Parvin Kumari) stating she had never seen her father (L225, L227, L230).

**Assessment:** The appellate record indicates the marriage evidence was insufficient to establish a legal union. The direct evidence was deemed unreliable, and the circumstantial evidence failed to meet the statutory requirements of Section 50 to overcome the denial of marriage by the daughter and the mother.

### 2. Property Provenance: Customary vs. Self-Acquired
The nature of the property determines the applicable succession law, which in turn dictates the heir's priority.

*   **Plaintiff's Claim:** The plaintiff alleged the properties were acquired by Ram Piari with the aid of his money and that he was governed by **Rajput custom** in Hoshiarpur, which grants the husband preferential succession over the daughter (L189, L190).
*   **Defendants' Claim:** The defendants (daughter and Raj Kumari) argued the properties were self-acquired by Ram Piari or given to her by Raj Kumari (a stranger), and that the alleged custom did not apply to such property (L190).
*   **High Court Reasoning:** The High Court accepted the defendants' argument regarding the provenance. It held that there was no local or general custom allowing the husband to succeed in preference to the daughter to property "left by Ram Piari which had been given to her by a stranger, namely, Raj Kumari" (L197). Consequently, the court applied **Hindu Law**, under which the daughter (Parvin Kumari) is entitled to succeed in preference to the plaintiff (L197).

**Assessment:** Even if the marriage were proven, the High Court determined that the specific provenance of the property (gift from a third party or self-acquired) excluded the application of the alleged Rajput custom favoring the husband, thereby elevating the daughter's priority under general Hindu law.

### 3. Conflicting Prior Findings
There is a direct conflict between the lower and higher courts regarding the foundational facts:
*   **Trial Court:** Found the marriage valid, the custom applicable, and the will invalid (L192, L194).
*   **High Court:** Found the marriage unproven, the custom inapplicable to the specific property, and the daughter the rightful heir under Hindu law (L196, L197).
*   **Appellate Status:** The current appeal seeks to overturn the High Court's decision. The record shows the High Court's findings on the lack of marriage evidence and the inapplicability of the custom were the grounds for dismissing the suit (L195).

### 4. Unresolved Legal Issues
Several critical legal issues remain unresolved or are the subject of the pending appeal:
*   **Validity of Marriage:** The ultimate question of whether the plaintiff proved a legal marriage remains the primary hurdle. Without a valid marriage, the claim to spousal inheritance priority collapses entirely.
*   **Applicability of Custom:** It remains legally unresolved whether the alleged Rajput custom of Hoshiarpur applies to property acquired with the husband's money versus property gifted by a third party. The High Court distinguished these, but the Supreme Court (in this appeal) must determine if that distinction holds.
*   **Validity of Will:** The trial court held the will invalid because the wife had no power to make one under the custom (L194). If the custom is found inapplicable, the question of whether the wife had testamentary capacity under Hindu law or general principles becomes relevant.

### Conclusion
The claimed spousal inheritance priority against the daughter is currently unsupported by the appellate record. The High Court's disposition rests on two pillars:
1.  **Failure to Prove Marriage:** The evidence of marriage was insufficient to meet the standards of the Indian Evidence Act (Section 50), being rebutted by the testimony of the mother and daughter.
2.  **Inapplicability of Custom:** Even assuming a marital relationship, the specific provenance of the property (gift from a stranger) precluded the application of the custom favoring the husband, leaving the daughter as the preferential heir under Hindu law.

The plaintiff's appeal challenges these findings, but based on the provided excerpts, the legal burden of proof regarding the marriage and the specific scope of the custom appears to have failed at the High Court level.
