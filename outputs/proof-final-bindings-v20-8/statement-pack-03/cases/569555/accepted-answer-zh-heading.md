## 摘要

Undisputed case fact and party admission (Tenancy history and trial proceedings): Pratap Chand, the owner and landlord of Room No. 6 in Pratap Buildings, Connaught Circus, New Delhi, let that room to the Automobile Association of Upper India, formerly known as the Automobile Association of Northern India. Karam Singh Sobti was a subtenant under the Association. The tenancy and subtenancy relationships were undisputed. The Association and Sobti admitted that Pratap Chand had not consented to the subletting before it began. Supreme court majority adopted reasoning and disposition with dissent distinguished (Supreme Court appeal and final disposition): The Supreme Court majority, comprising S. K. Das, Acting Chief Justice, and M. Hidayatullah, J., held that Pratap Chand was entitled to rely on section 13(1), proviso (c)(i), of the Delhi and Ajmer Rent Control Act, 1952, to recover possession of Room No. 6 from the tenancy involving the Association and Sobti. The subletting had commenced before the Act came into force on June 9, 1952, prior landlord consent had not been obtained, and Sobti had failed to establish subsequent acquiescence by Pratap Chand. The majority held that section 57(2) of the Delhi Rent Control Act, 1958, and its first proviso did not displace the applicable 1952 statutory exception. It upheld the Punjab High Court's restoration of the trial court's possession decree and dismissed Sobti's appeal with costs. Sarkar J. dissented on the transitional statutory interpretation.

## 依据

STATUTORY_PROVISION_AND_LANDLORD_PLEADED_GROUND; Trial suit and Supreme Court examination of the 1952 statutory ground [S03]

Section 13(1) of the Delhi and Ajmer Rent Control Act, 1952, generally prohibited a court from ordering recovery of possession by a landlord against a tenant, subject to specified exceptions. Proviso (c)(i) made the prohibition inapplicable where the tenant, without obtaining the landlord's consent, had sublet, assigned, or otherwise parted with possession of the whole or part of the premises before the commencement of that Act. Pratap Chand expressly invoked this exception as the legal basis for ejectment concerning Room No. 6.

On August 26, 1957, Pratap Chand applied to the High Court of Punjab for revision under section 35 of the 1952 Act. PROCEDURAL_HISTORY_STATUTORY_CONTEXT_AND_MAJORITY_ISSUE_FRAMING; Pending Punjab High Court revision and Supreme Court statutory interpretation [S14]

The 1958 Delhi Rent Control Act came into force on February 9, 1959, while Pratap Chand's revision was pending in the Punjab High Court. Section 57(1) repealed the 1952 Act insofar as it applied to Delhi, while section 57(2) preserved pending proceedings under the repealed Act. Its first proviso directed courts dealing with specified rent and eviction proceedings concerning premises outside section 54 to have regard to the provisions of the 1958 Act. The Supreme Court majority treated the interaction between that saving clause and the first proviso as decisive for whether the landlord could continue relying on the 1952 subletting exception.

SUPREME_COURT_MAJORITY_ADOPTED_STATUTORY_INTERPRETATION; Supreme Court appeal, section 57(2) interpretation [S16]

The Supreme Court majority construed the first proviso to section 57(2) harmoniously with the saving clause. Under its interpretation, courts should take account of provisions of the 1958 Act that slightly modified or clarified the corresponding earlier law, but materially new rights or liabilities under the 1958 Act should not displace the rights preserved for pending proceedings under the 1952 Act. The majority expressly agreed with that approach and rejected wholesale retrospective substitution of the 1958 eviction regime. Accordingly, the 1952 Act's pre-commencement unauthorized-subletting exception remained available to Pratap Chand.

SUPREME_COURT_MAJORITY_ADOPTED_REVISIONAL_EVALUATION; Supreme Court appeal reviewing Punjab High Court revision [S10]

The Supreme Court majority agreed that the Punjab High Court could interfere under section 35 of the 1952 Act where the lower appellate finding lacked any supporting evidence. A finding unsupported by evidence may mean that the decision was not made according to law, whereas revision does not ordinarily permit mere reassessment of the weight of evidence supporting a factual finding. The majority agreed that the lower appellate inference of Pratap Chand's acquiescence was unsupported and that the High Court's intervention was justified.

SUPREME_COURT_MAJORITY_ADOPTED_EVIDENTIARY_EVALUATION; Supreme Court appeal, evaluation of acquiescence [S11]

The Supreme Court majority accepted that Pratap Chand knew Sobti physically occupied Room No. 6, but agreed that such knowledge did not necessarily prove knowledge that Sobti occupied under a contractual subtenancy. The evidence and circumstances did not support the inference that Pratap Chand had acquiesced in the subletting. This was a failure of proof of subtenancy knowledge and acquiescence, not an affirmative finding that the landlord was unaware of Sobti's physical occupation.

The circumstances supporting the rejection of acquiescence included Sobti's occupation of the room alongside the Association from sometime in 1949 until November 1950, his publication of the Association's official magazine under an agreement providing office accommodation in Room No. 6, and his former position as the Association's General Secretary. After the Association moved to other premises in November 1950, Sobti remained in sole occupation but continued publishing its official magazine. Sarkar J. reasoned, on a point accepted by the majority, that the landlord might have understood the earlier occupation as connected with the Association and that the evidence did not establish knowledge of subtenancy when rent was received.

LANDLORD_ARGUMENT_REJECTED_AND_SUPREME_COURT_MAJORITY_AGREED_PROCEDURAL_HOLDING; High Court revision and Supreme Court appeal [S13]

During the Punjab High Court revision, Pratap Chand also argued that the Association's failure to appeal the joint trial ejectment decree meant that the tenancy had ended and Sobti, whose rights derived through the Association, could not remain in possession. The High Court rejected the asserted automatic consequence of the Association's nonappeal. The Supreme Court majority agreed with Sarkar J. that the trial court had made one decree against both the Association and Sobti, that Sobti had his own right to appeal, and that the Association's failure to appeal did not extinguish that independent appellate right. This procedural holding did not itself establish Sobti's substantive right to retain possession.

## 相反观点与处理

SUBTENANT_PARTY_ARGUMENT_NOT_MAJORITY_ADOPTED; Trial defence and subsequent appellate dispute [S07]

Sobti opposed ejectment by contending that Pratap Chand knew that Sobti occupied the room as a subtenant, continued accepting rent from the Association with that knowledge, and thereby acquiesced in the subletting. This was Sobti's contested defence, not an admission by the landlord or an established finding of the Supreme Court majority.

LOWER_APPELLATE_FINDING_AND_REVERSAL_LATER_REJECTED_AS_TO_ACQUIESCENCE; Lower appeal, June 11, 1957, followed by revision [S08]

On Sobti's appeal, the Additional Senior Subordinate Judge of Delhi found that subletting had commenced not later than November 1950 and that Pratap Chand thereafter continued receiving rent with full knowledge of the subletting. On that basis, the lower appellate court held that the landlord could not invoke section 13(1), proviso (c)(i), allowed Sobti's appeal, set aside the trial judgment, and dismissed the suit on June 11, 1957. The inference concerning knowledge and acquiescence was subsequently rejected by the Punjab High Court and was not adopted by the Supreme Court majority.

SUBTENANT_STATUTORY_COUNTERARGUMENT_AND_MAJORITY_ACKNOWLEDGED_COUNTERFACTUAL_NOT_ADOPTED; Supreme Court appeal, competing 1952 and 1958 statutory grounds [S15]

Sobti's decisive statutory opposition was that the pending revision should be decided according to the substantive provisions of the 1958 Act rather than the 1952 Act. The Supreme Court majority acknowledged that, if the 1958 Act governed the merits, its section 14(1), proviso (b), would prevent Pratap Chand from relying on the identified unauthorized-subletting ground, because the subletting occurred before June 9, 1952, rather than on or after that date. This was an expressly considered alternative legal consequence, not the majority's selected governing-law conclusion.

SARKAR_J_DISSENTING_LEGAL_INTERPRETATION_AND_PROPOSED_DISPOSITION; Supreme Court appeal, dissenting opinion [S17]

Sarkar J., dissenting from the majority's interpretation of section 57(2), considered that the first proviso required the substantive provisions of the 1958 Act to be applied to pending tenant-eviction proceedings. He rejected limiting the proviso to provisions merely clarifying or slightly modifying the 1952 Act. He also interpreted the statutory reference to suits and proceedings as including appeals and revisions. Applying that interpretation, he concluded that the subletting to Sobti, which preceded June 9, 1952, could not support possession under the unauthorized-subletting ground in section 14(1), proviso (b), of the 1958 Act. He would have allowed the appeal. These propositions were Sarkar J.'s dissenting reasoning and proposed disposition, not the Supreme Court majority's adopted result.

Sarkar J. separately considered the appellant's argument that section 16(1) of the 1958 Act protected the subletting. ## 未决与裁判范围

The Supreme Court majority expressly found it unnecessary to determine the separate interpretive question whether the expression 'suits and proceedings' in section 57(2), considered with its second proviso, included pending revision applications. Sarkar J. addressed that question affirmatively in his dissent, but his reasoning cannot be reported as an additional majority holding. The majority resolved the appeal through its interpretation of the first proviso and the continued availability of the 1952 statutory ground.

A further legal argument was raised that even if Pratap Chand's acquiescence in the Association's subletting to Sobti had been proved, it would not affect the landlord's statutory entitlement arising from the original absence of consent. Sarkar J. expressly declined to decide that hypothetical legal proposition because acquiescence had not been proved. The majority agreed with the rejection of acquiescence on the evidence, but the judgment supplies no adopted determination of the hypothetical effect of legally proved acquiescence.

The lower appellate court's finding that Pratap Chand accepted rent with knowledge of Sobti's subtenancy must remain attributed to that court. The Supreme Court's evidentiary discussion distinguished knowledge of physical occupation from knowledge of contractual subtenancy and rejected the inference of acquiescence on the available evidence. The landlord's alleged date of first learning about subletting was a contested assertion, and this judgment does not establish a precise date of such knowledge.

The recorded conditional results for the supplied rules do not independently establish a completed entitlement calculation: the 1952 statutory exception, the section 35 revisional standard, and the section 57(2) transitional interpretation each require attention to their specified statutory scope and the judgment's own legal reasoning. The Supreme Court majority's actual conclusion in this case is separately stated in its reasons and disposition. Neither a TRUE individual premise nor the final decree alone should be used to infer resolution of every hypothetical right, factual dispute, or issue expressly left open.

The majority expressly declined to base its decision on the cited Punjab High Court theory that the first proviso to section 57(2) was merely directory and conferred a general discretion to consider the new Act. The majority instead adopted its own construction of section 57(2) and the first proviso.

Sarkar J. expressly left undecided whether landlord Pratap Chand could obtain possession on any other ground under the 1958 Act, because that question did not arise in this appeal. His dissenting proposed allowance of the appeal did not adjudicate those other grounds.
