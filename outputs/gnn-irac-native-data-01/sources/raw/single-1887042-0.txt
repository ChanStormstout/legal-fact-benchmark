L83:     HEADNOTE:
L84:          The appellant's  husband was the tenant in respect of a
L85:     shop under  the respondent-landlord  since 1979. In 1970 the
L86:     respondent-landlord  served  a	notice	on  the	 appellant's
L87:     husband determining  the tenancy.  Thereafter,	he  filed  a
L88:     petition under	cite15†section 14 of the  Delhi Rent	Control Act,
L89:     1958 for  eviction of  the tenant  from	 the  said  shop  on
L90:     several grounds	 including the grounds of nonpayment of rent
L91:     and sub-letting. The Rent Controller dismissed the petition.
L92:     Against the  order of  the Rent	 Controller the	 respondent-
L93:     landlord  preferred   an  appeal  before  the  Rent  Control
L94:     Tribunal and  the tenant  filed cross-objections. During the
L95:     pendency of  the appeal,  the tenant  died and	the  present
L96:     appellant  was	 substituted.  The   Rent  Control  Tribunal
L97:     remanded the  case to  the Rent	 Controller  to	 decide	 the
L98:     question  of   sub-letting   afresh   after   affording	  an
L99:     opportunity to the parties to lead evidence.
L100:          Aggrieved by  the order  of the  Rent Control Tribunal,
L101:     the appellant  filed  an  appeal  in  the  High	 Court.	 The
L102:     respondent-landlord filed cross-objection and further raised
L103:     a contention  that in  view of	the death  of  the  original
L104:     tenant who  continued to remain in possession of the shop as
L105:     a statutory tenant, the widow and the heirs of the deceased-
L106:     tenant were not entitled to continue to remain in occupation
L107:     thereof. The High Court allowed the cross-objection filed by
L108:     the respondent-landlord	 and passed  a decree  for  eviction
L109:     against	 the   appellant  mainly  on  the  ground  that	 the
L110:     protection afforded  to the  statutory tenant by the Act was
L111:     not available to the heirs and the legal representatives.
L112:          In appeal	to this	 Court the  appellant while  relying
L113:     upon Damadi  Lal and Ors. v. Parashram and Ors. [1976] Supp.
L114:     S.C.R. 645  and V. Dhanapal Chettiar v. Yesodai Ammal [1980]
L115:     1   S.C.R.   334,   contended	that   notwithstanding	 the
L116:     determination of  the statutory	 tenancy of  the  tenant  in
L117:     respect of  any commercial  premises, the  position  in	 law
L118:     remains unchanged  in so  far as  the tenancy  in respect of
L119:     commercial premises is concerned by virtue of the provisions
L120:     of the	Act. The  respondent, however  argued (i)  that	 the
L121:     protection against the eviction after termination of tenancy
L122:     afforded to  a tenant by the Act creates a personal right in
L123:     favour of  the tenant  who continues to remain in possession
L124:     after termination  of his  tenancy  without  any  estate  or
L125:     interest in
L126:     2
L127:     the premises and therefore on the death of such a tenant his
L128:     heirs who  had	 neither  any  estate  or  interest  in	 the
L129:     tenanted premises  and who  do not have any protection under
L130:     the Act	 against eviction  are liable  to be  evicted  as  a
L131:     matter of  course under	 the ordinary  law of  the land; and
L132:     (ii) that  the amendment  to the definition of 'tenant' with
L133:     retrospective effect  introduced by  the cite14†Delhi Rent Control Amendment Act (Act 18	of 1976) to give personal protection
L134:     and personal right to continue in possession to the heirs of
L135:     the deceased  statutory tenant	in  respect  of	 residential
L136:     premises only and not with regard to the so called statutory
L137:     tenant in  respect of commercial premises indicated that the
L138:     heirs of  so called  statutory	tenant,	 do  not  enjoy	 any
L139:     protection under the Act.

L144:          HELD: (1)(i)  The term  "statutory tenant"	 is used  in
L145:     cite16†English Rent Act and though this term is not to be found in
L146:     the Indian  Acts, in  the judgments of the Supreme Court and
L147:     also various  High Courts in India, this term has often been
L148:     used to	 denote a  tenant whose contractual tenancy has been
L149:     terminated but who has become entitled to continue to remain
L150:     in possession by virtue of the protection afforded to him by
L151:     the statutes  in question;  namely, the various Rent Control
L152:     Acts prevailing	 in different  States of  India. It  is also
L153:     important to  note that	 notwithstanding the  termination of
L154:     the contractual	 tenancy by  the  Landlord,  the  tenant  is
L155:     afforded protection  against eviction  and is  permitted  to
L156:     continue to  remain in possession even after the termination
L157:     of the	contractual tenancy  by	 the  Act  in  question	 and
L158:     invariably by  all the	Rent Acts in force in various States
L159:     so long	 as an	order of  decree for  eviction	against	 the
L160:     tenant on  any of  the grounds specified in such Acts on the
L161:     basis of  which an  order or decree for eviction against the
L162:     tenant can be passed, is not passed. [14H; 15 A-B]
L163:          (1)(ii) Though  provisions of all the Rent Control Acts
L164:     are not	 uniform, the common feature of all the Rent Control
L165:     Legislation is	that a contractual tenant on the termination
L166:     of the contractual tenancy is by virtue of the provisions of
L167:     the Rent Acts not liable to be evicted as a matter of course
L168:     under the  ordinary law	 of the	 land and  he is entitled to
L169:     remain	in   possession	 even  after  determination  of	 the
L170:     contractual tenancy and no order or decree for eviction will
L171:     be passed  against a tenant unless any ground which entitles
L172:     the landlord  go get  an  order	 or  decree  for  possession
L173:     specified in the Act is established. [25D-E]
L174:          (2)(i) It	is clear  from	the  definition	 of  tenant,
L175:     whether in  the original Act or in the amended Act, that the
L176:     tenant within  the meaning  of the definition of the term in
L177:     the Act	 includes any  person continuing in possession after
L178:     the termination of his tenancy. [28 G]
L179:          (2)(ii) cite15†Section 14 of  the Act clearly postulates that
L180:     despite the  termination of  the tenancy and notwithstanding
L181:     the provisions	of any	other  law  which  might  have	been
L182:     applicable on  the termination	of the	contractual tenancy,
L183:     protection against eviction is applicable to every tenant as
L184:     defined
L185:     3
L186:     in cite17†S. 2(i) of the Act. This section clearly establishes that
L187:     determination of  a contractual	 tenancy does not disqualify
L188:     him from  continuing to	 be a  tenant within  the meaning of
L189:     this Act  and the  tenant whose contractual tenancy has been
L190:     determined enjoys  the same  position  and  is	entitled  to
L191:     protection against  eviction. The  other sections in Chapter
L192:     III also  go to	 indicate that	the tenant whose tenancy has
L193:     been terminated	 enjoys the  same status  and benefit  as  a
L194:     tenant whose  tenancy has  not been terminated, and a tenant
L195:     after termination  of his tenancy stands on the same footing
L196:     as the	tenant before  such termination. Chapter III A which
L197:     provides for  summary trial  for certain  applications	also
L198:     does not make any distinction between a tenant whose tenancy
L199:     has been  determined and a tenant whose tenancy has not been
L200:     terminated. Chapter  IV which  deals with  deposit  of	rent
L201:     consists of  ss. 26  to 29  and these sections make it clear
L202:     that the  tenant after determination of a tenancy is treated
L203:     under the  Act on the same footing as a tenant whose tenancy
L204:     has not been determined. Chapter VI though not very material
L205:     for the	 purpose  of  adjudication  of	the  point  involved
L206:     indicates that	no discrimination  is made  in the matter of
L207:     proceedings for	 eviction between  the 'so  called statutory
L208:     tenant' and a contractual tenant. Chapter VII which consists
L209:     of cite18†sections 44 to cite19†49 makes provisions regarding obligations
L210:     of landlords  and also provides for penalties in appropriate
L211:     cases. The  sections make  it  clear  that  the	 duties	 and
L212:     obligations  of	 landlords  cast  upon	the  landlord  apply
L213:     equally whether the tenant is a so called 'statutory tenant'
L214:     or the	tenant is  a contractual tenant. It may, however, be
L215:     noted that  cite20†section 50 which bars the jurisdiction of Civil
L216:     Courts in  respect of  certain matters	does not  in any way
L217:     discriminate between  a 'so  called statutory  tenant' and a
L218:     contractual tenant. Thus, the various provisions of the Act,
L219:     make it	 abundantly clear  that the  Act does  not make	 any
L220:     distinction between  a 'so  called statutory  tenant' and  a
L221:     'contractual tenant'  and the  Act proceeds  to	 treat	both
L222:     alike and to preserve and protect the status and rights of a
L223:     tenant after determination of the contractual tenancy in the
L224:     same way  as the  status and  rights of a contractual tenant
L225:     are protected and preserved. [30 A-H; 31A]
L226:          V. Dhanpal Chattiar v. Yesodai Ammal, [1980] 1 SCR 334,
L227:     relied upon.
L228:          (3)(i) The	 termination of	 the contractual  tenancy in
L229:     view of the definition of 'tenant' in the Act does not bring
L230:     about any  change in  the status  and legal  position of the
L231:     tenant, unless	there are  contrary provisions	in the	Act;
L232:     and, the  tenant notwithstanding  the termination of tenancy
L233:     does enjoy  an estate  or interest in the tenanted premises.
L234:     This interest  or estate  which the  tenant  under  the	 Act
L235:     despite termination  of the contractual tenancy continues to
L236:     enjoy creates  a heritable  interest in	 the absence  of any
L237:     provision to  the contrary.  The amendment of the definition
L238:     of 'tenant'  by Act  18	 of  1976  introducing	particularly
L239:     cite17†section 2(i)(iii) does not in any way mitigate against this
L240:     view.  The   said  sub-section	(iii)  with  all  the  three
L241:     Explanations thereto  is not in any way inconsistent with or
L242:     contrary  to   sub-section  (ii)   of  cite21†Section 2(1) which
L243:     unequivocally  states  that  'tenant'  includes	 any  person
L244:     continuing  in	possession  after  the	termination  of	 his
L245:     tenancy. In  the absence  of the provision contained in sub-
L246:     cite17†section 2(i)(iii) ,  the heritable  interest of	the heirs of
L247:     the statutory  tenant would  devolve on all the heirs of the
L248:     'so called  statutory tenant'  on his death and the heirs of
L249:     such tenant would in law step into his position. [33 G-H; 33
L250:     A-C]
L251:          Damadial & Ors. v. Parashram & Ors. [1976] Supp, S.C.R.
L252:     645 followed.
L253:     4
L254:          (3)(ii) cite17†Section 2(i)(iii) of  the Act  does not create
L255:     any additional	or special  right in  favour of the heirs of
L256:     the 'so	 called statutory tenant' on his death, but seeks to
L257:     restrict the right of the heirs of such tenant in respect of
L258:     residential  premises.	 As  the  status  and  rights  of  a
L259:     contractual tenant  even after	determination of his tenancy
L260:     when the  tenant is  at times  described  as  the  statutory
L261:     tenant, are fully protected by the Act and the heirs of such
L262:     tenants become	entitled by  virtue of the provisions of the
L263:     Act to	inherit the  status and	 position of  the  statutory
L264:     tenant on  his death, the Legislature which has created this
L265:     right has thought it fit in the case of residential premises
L266:     to limit  the rights  of the  heirs in the manner and to the
L267:     extent provided	 in cite22†S. 2(1)(iii) . However,  the Legislature
L268:     has not	 thought it  fit to  put any  such restrictions with
L269:     regard to  tenants in respect of commercial premises in this
L270:     Act. So	 long as  the contractual  tenancy of  a tenant	 who
L271:     carries on  the business continues, there can be no question
L272:     of the	heirs of the deceased tenant not only inheriting the
L273:     tenancy but  also  inheriting  the  business  and  they	 are
L274:     entitled to run and enjoy the same. [33 D-G]
L275:          (3)(iii) The mere fact that in the Act no provision has
L276:     been made  with regard to the heirs of tenants in respect of
L277:     commercial tenancies  on  the  death  of  the  tenant  after
L278:     termination of	the tenancy, as has been done in the case of
L279:     heirs of  the tenants  of  residential	premises,  does	 not
L280:     indicate that the Legislature intended that the heirs of the
L281:     tenants of  commercial premises	 will  cease  to  enjoy	 the
L282:     protection  afforded  to  the  tenant  under  the  Act.	 The
L283:     Legislature could  never have  possible intended  that	with
L284:     death of  a tenant  of the commercial premises, the business
L285:     carried on  by the tenant, however flourishing it may be and
L286:     even if the same constituted the source of livelihood of the
L287:     members of  the family,	 must necessarily  come to an end on
L288:     the death  of the  tenant only because the tenant died after
L289:     the contractual tenancy had been terminated. [35 F-H]
L290:          (3)(iv) In	 case of commercial premises governed by the
L291:     cite23†Delhi Act the Legislature  has not  thought to	 fit in	 the
L292:     light of  the situation	 at  Delhi  to	place  any  kind  of
L293:     restriction on	the ordinary  law of inheritance with regard
L294:     to succession.	It may also be borne in mind that in case of
L295:     commercial premises  the heirs	of the	deceased tenant	 not
L296:     only succeed  to the  tenancy right in the premises but they
L297:     succeed to  the business as a whole. It might have been open
L298:     to the	Legislature  to	 limit	or  restrict  the  right  of
L299:     inheritance with  regard to  the tenancy  as the Legislature
L300:     had done  in the  case of  the tenancies  with regard to the
L301:     residential houses  but it  would not  have been open to the
L302:     Legislature  to	 alter	under  the  cite24†Rent Act ,	the  law  of
L303:     succession  regarding  the  business  which  is	 a  valuable
L304:     heritable right	 and which  must necessarily  devolve on all
L305:     the heirs  in  accordance  with	 law.  The  absence  of	 any
L306:     provision restricting  the heritability	 of the	 tenancy  in
L307:     respect of  the commercial  premises only  establishes	that
L308:     commercial tenancies  notwithstanding the  determination  of
L309:     the contractual	 tenancies will	 devolve  on  the  heirs  in
L310:     accordance with law and the heirs who step into the position
L311:     of the deceased tenant will continue to enjoy the protection
L312:     afforded by  the  act  and  they  can  only  be	 evicted  in
L313:     accordance with the provisions of the Act. [36 B-E]
L314:     5
L315:          (3)(v) As	the protection afforded by the cite24†Rent Act to a
L316:     tenant after  determination of	the tenancy and to his heirs
L317:     on the death of such tenant is a creation of the Act for the
L318:     benefit of  the tenants, it is open to the Legislature which
L319:     provides for  such protection to make appropriate provisions
L320:     in the	Act with  regard to  the nature	 and extent  of	 the
L321:     benefit and protection to be enjoyed and the manner in which
L322:     the same  is to	 be enjoyed.  If the  Legislature makes	 any
L323:     provision in the Act limiting or restricting the benefit and
L324:     the nature  of the  protection to  be enjoyed in a specified
L325:     manner by  any particular  class of  heirs of  the  deceased
L326:     tenant on  any condition  laid	down  being  fulfilled,	 the
L327:     benefit of  the protection  has necessarily to be enjoyed on
L328:     the fulfillment	 of the	 condition in  the manner and to the
L329:     extent stipulated  in the  Act. The Legislature which by the
L330:     cite24†Rent Act seeks to  confer the benefit on the tenants and to
L331:     afford protection  against eviction,  is perfectly competent
L332:     to make	 appropriate  provision	 regulating  the  nature  of
L333:     protection and	the manner  and extent	of enjoyment of such
L334:     tenancy rights	after the termination of contractual tenancy
L335:     of the	tenant	including  the	rights	and  the  nature  of
L336:     protection of the heirs on the death of the tenant. [38 C-F]
L337:          (3) (vi)  When the tenant is a Company or a Corporation
L338:     or any body with juristic personality, question of the death
L339:     of the tenant will not arise. Despite the termination of the
L340:     tenancy, the  Company or  the Corporation  of such  juristic
L341:     personalities, however,	 will go  on enjoying the protection
L342:     afforded to  the tenant	 under the  Act. It  can  hardly  be
L343:     conceived that	the Legislature	 would intend to deny to one
L344:     class of  tenants, namely,  individuals the protection which
L345:     will be	 enjoyed by the other class, namely, the Corporation
L346:     and Companies  and other  bodies with  juristic	 personality
L347:     under the Act. [36 G-H]
L348:          (4) In  the instant  case there  is no provision in the
L349:     Act regulating	the rights  of	the  heirs  to	inherit	 the
L350:     tenancy rights	of the	tenant in  respect of  the  tenanted
L351:     premises which	is commercial  premises, the  tenancy  right
L352:     which is  heritable devolves on the heirs under the ordinary
L353:     law of	succession. The tenancy right of appellant's husband
L354:     therefore, devolves on all the heirs on his death. The heirs
L355:     and legal  representatives of  appellant's husband step into
L356:     his position  and they	are  entitled  to  the	benefit	 and
L357:     protection of  the Act.	 Therefore, the	 High Court  was not
L358:     right  in  coming  to  the  conclusion	that  the  heirs  of
L359:     appellant's husband  the so called statutory tenant, did not
L360:     have any  right to  remain in  possession  of  the  tenanted
L361:     premises and did not enjoy any protection under the Act. The
L362:     judgment and  order of the High Court is therefore set aside
L363:     and the	 case is  remanded to the High Court for decision of
L364:     the appeal  and the cross objection on merits. The appeal is
L365:     accordingly allowed to the extent indicated above. [39 B; D-
L366:     E; G]
L367:          Ganpat Ladha  v. Sashikant Vishnu Shinde [1978] 3 S. C.
L368:     R. 198 dissented.
L369:          The Legislature may consider the advisability of making
L370:     bona fide  requirement of  the landlord a ground of eviction
L371:     in respect of commercial premises as well. [40 F]

L373:     Per Bhagwati, J. (Concurring)
L374:          Though genetically	 the  parentage	 of  the  two  legal
L375:     conceits, namely,  contractual tenancy and statutory tenancy
L376:     is different, one owing its origin to contract and the other
L377:     to rent	 control legislation,  they are	 equated  with	each
L378:     other and  their incidents  are the  same. If  a contractual
L379:     tenant has  an estate  or interest  in the premises which is
L380:     heritable, it  is difficult  to understand  why a  statutory
L381:     tenant should  be held	not to have such heritable estate or
L382:     interest. In  one case, the estate or interest is the result
L383:     of contract while in the other, it is the result of statute.
L384:     But the	 quality of  the estate	 or interest  is the same in
L385:     both cases.  When the  rent  control  legislation  places  a
L386:     statutory tenant  on  the  same	 footing  as  a	 contractual
L387:     tenant, wipes  out  the	 distinction  between  the  two	 and
L388:     invests a  statutory tenant with the same right, obligations
L389:     and incidents  as a  contractual tenant,  why should  it  be
L390:     difficult to  hold that,  just like  a contractual tenant, a
L391:     statutory tenant also has estate or interest in the premises
L392:     which can be inherited. [8 B-F]
L393:          It is  true that  there  are  certain  observations  in
L394:     Ganpat Ladha  v. Sashikant  Vishnu Shinde, [1978] 3 S. C. R.
L395:     198, which  go counter	to what	 the Court is holding in the
L396:     present case  and to  that extent these observations must be

L428: The following Judgments were delivered BHAGWATI, J. I entirely agree with the Judgment just delivered by my learned brother A.N. Sen, J. I am adding a few words of my own since I was a party to the decision in cite28†Ganpat Ladha v. Shashikant Vishnu Shinde (1) where certain observations were made which seem to take a different view from the one we are taking in the present case.
L429: The question which arises here for consideration is as to whether statutory tenancy is heritable on the death of the statutory tenant. 'Statutory tenant' is not an expression to be found in any provision of the cite24†Delhi Rent Control Act 1958 or the rent control legislation of any other State.
L430: It is an expression coined by the judges in England and, like many other concepts in English law, it has been imported into the jurisprudence of this country and has become an expression of common use to denote a tenant whose contractual tenancy has been determined but who is continuing in possession of the premises by virtue of the protection against eviction afforded to him by the rent control legislation.
L431: Though the expression 'statutory tenant' has not been used in any rent control legislation the concept of statutory tenant finds recognition in almost every rent control legislation. The definition of 'tenant' in cite26†Section 2(1) of the Delhi Rent Control Act 1958-and I am referring here to the provisions of the cite24†Delhi Rent Control Act 1958 because that is the statute with which we are concerned in the present case-includes a statutory tenant.
L432: It says in clause (ii) that 'tenant' includes any person continuing the possession after the termination of his tenancy'. Such a person would not be a tenant under the ordinary law but he is recognised as a 'tenant' by the rent control legislation and is therefore described as a statutory tenant as contra-distinguished from contractual tenant.
L433: The statutory tenant is, by virtue of inclusion in the definition of 'tenant', placed on the same footing as contractual tenant so far as rent control legislation is concerned. The rent control legislation in fact, as pointed out by this Court in a seven judge Bench decision in cite29†V. Dhanapal Chettiar v. Yesodai Ammal (2) does not make any distinction between contractual tenant and statutory tenant.
