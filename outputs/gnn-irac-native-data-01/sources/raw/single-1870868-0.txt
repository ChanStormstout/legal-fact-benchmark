L85:          The  respondent   landlord	 let  out  the	building  in
L86:     question to  the appellant tenant in the year 1954, when the
L87:     cite12†Transfer of Property Act was not applicable to Delhi where
L88:     the property  is situated.  cite12†The Transfer of Property Act was
L89:     made applicable	 to Delhi  in the  year 1962.  In 1967,	 the
L90:     respondent filed  a suit  for eviction against the appellant
L91:     without	 terminating  the  tenancy  under  the	cite12†Transfer of Property Act on the  grounds of unauthorised subletting and
L92:     acquisition of	alternative accommodation  by the  tenant. A
L93:     decree for  eviction was passed by the Rent Controller which
L94:     was affirmed by the Appellate Tribunal.
L95:          In the  High Court	 it was	 contended by  the appellant
L96:     that  neither  notice  to  quit	 nor  notice  of  forfeiture
L97:     determining  the  tenancy  was	given  by  the	landlord  as
L98:     required by cite13†sections 106 and cite14†111 of the Transfer of Property
L99:     Act. The  respondent contended that the lease had expired by
L100:     efflux	of   time  under   cite15†section 111(a) and  no  notice
L101:     terminating the tenancy was necessary and that forfeiture of
L102:     the tenancy  caused by	the subletting contrary to the terms
L103:     of the	agreement can  be availed of by the landlord even in
L104:     the absence  of a  notice as contemplated by cite16†section 111(g) .
L105:     The High Court dismissed the petition filed by the appellant
L106:     but granted  a certificate  of	fitness	 under	cite17†Article 133 L107:     restricting it	to one	ground urged  before the High Court.
L108:     The respondent raised a preliminary objection that since the
L109:     certificate was	 granted only  on one  point  the  appellant
L110:     could not be permitted to make any other submissions.
L111:          The appellant contended that the lease is one where the
L112:     time is not limited and, therefore, is terminable only by 15
L113:     days notice  as required  by  cite13†section 106 of	Transfer  of
L114:     Property Act.
L115:          The respondent contended that the lease was for a fixed
L116:     period and  expired by	efflux of time. In any evnt a notice
L117:     in  writing   is  not  necessary  to  terminate	 the  lease.
L118:     Institution of legal proceedings serves that purpose.
L119:     ^
L120:          HELD: (1)	Once  a	 certificate  of  fitness  has	been
L121:     granted under cite17†Article 133 , the appeal, in all its amplitude,
L122:     is before  the Court  and every	 point may  be urged  by the
L123:     appellant provided  this Court	permits it  having regard to
L124:     the  circumstances.   It  is  however,	within	the  court's
L125:     discretion not to allow a new point to be taken up. [909D-E]
L126:          (2) The  scheme of	 the Rent  Control Law,	 is  to	 put
L127:     further fetters	 on landlords  seeking eviction where in the
L128:     absence of  such  acute	 barriers  the	landlords  would  be
L129:     entitled to  ejectment. Even  where under  a particular Rent
L130:     Control Statute	 the landlord makes out grounds for eviction
L131:     he can	institute proceedings in this behalf only if de hors
L132:     the said  grounds he  has cause of action under the cite12†Transfer of Property Act . The  landlord cannot	secure an  order for
L133:     eviction without  first establishing  that  he	has  validly
L134:     determined the	lease under  the transfer  of cite18†Property Act .
L135:     [909G-H,911C]
L136:          (3) A lease merely stating that it is for a period less
L137:     than one  year is  ex-facie for	 an indefinite period and as
L138:     such cannot expire by efflux of time.
L139:     907
L140:     Nor are	 we convinced  that the	 acceptance of	rent for the
L141:     period of  11 years does not amount assenting to the holding
L142:     over of the tenancy by the landlord. [911E-F]
L143:          (4) cite19†The Rent Act contemplates no elaborate proceedings
L144:     but filing  out of the particulars in a proforma which takes
L145:     the place  of a	 plaint. No  specific averment of forfeiture
L146:     and consequent	determination of  the lease  is found in the
L147:     petition. The  question arises	whether a  written notice of
L148:     forfeiture for	the breach  of the condition of the lease is
L149:     obligatory in  terms of	 cite16†section 111(g) or whether  written
L150:     notice of  forfeiture can be dispensed with as being no part
L151:     of the equity or justice but a technical or formal statutory
L152:     requirement. Before  the amendment  of cite12†Transfer of Property Act in	1929 all  that	was  necessary	for  the  lessor  to
L153:     determine the  demise on  forfeiture  was  to  do  some	 act
L154:     showing his  intention to  determine the  lease. The rule of
L155:     English Law'before the enactment of the law of Property Act,
L156:     1925, appears  to be that a suit for ejectment is equivalent
L157:     to a  re-entry. The appellant did not urge in the High Court
L158:     that the  cite12†Transfer of Property Act was applicable in its own
L159:     force. We  decline our	discretion to allow the appellant to
L160:     travel into  the new statutory territory of cite16†section 111 (g) .
L161:     [911G-H, 913A, D-E, 916C]
L162:          (5) In  India and	in  other  colonies  throughout	 the
L163:     Imperial Era  a tacit assumption had persuaded the courts to
L164:     embrace English	 Law (the civilizing mission of the masters)
L165:     as justice,  equity and good conscience. Unfortunately, even
L166:     after liberation,  this neo  colonial jurisprudence  was not
L167:     shaken off.  Free India	 has to	 find its  conscience in our
L168:     rugged realities  and no  more in  alien legal	thought.  So
L169:     viewed, the  basic question is what is the essence of equity
L170:     in the	matter of determination of a lease on the grounds of
L171:     forfeiture  caused   by	 the  breach  of  a  condition.	 The
L172:     substance of  the matter-the  justice  of  the	situation-is
L173:     whether a  condition in	 the lease  has	 been  breached	 and
L174:     whether the lessor has by some overt act brought home to the
L175:     lessee his  election to eject on the strength of the breach.
L176:     The touchstone	is simply  whether the formal requirement of
L177:     the law	 is part of what is necessarily just and reasonable.
L178:     In this perspective the conclusion is clear that a notice in
L179:     writing formally  determining the  tenancy is  not a rule of
L180:     justice or  cannon of commonsense. Realism married to equity
L181:     being the  true test, we are persuaded that pre-amending Act
L182:     provision of  cite16†section 111 (g) is in consonance with justice.
L183:     The mere  institution of  the legal  proceeding for eviction
L184:     fulfills the  requirements of  law for	determination of the
L185:     lease. The  conscience of  the Court  needs nothing more and
L186:     nothing less.  The essential  principles, not  the technical
L187:     rules, of the cite12†Transfer of Property Act form part of justice,
L188:     equity and  good conscience.  [916D,  917A,  D,	 E-F,919B-C,
L189:     920A]

L193:     JUDGMENT:
L194: CIVIL APPELLATE JURISDICTION: Civil Appeal No. 1297 of 1975.
L195: 
L196: From the judgment and order/decree dated the 30th May, 1975 of the Delhi High Court in S.A.O. 43 of 1973.
L197: 
L198: A. K. Sen, R. L. Kohli, S. K. Bagga, Mrs. S. Bagga and Miss Yash Bagga for the Appellant.
L199: 
L200: Y. S. Chitaley, R. P. Singh, R. K. Jain and M. Mudgal for Respondent No. 1.
L201: The Judgment of the Court was delivered by KRISHNA IYER,J. This fifth deck appeal, by certificate under cite17†Art. 133 of the Constitution, stems from a humdrum but protected litigation under the rent control law by a tenant who has lost all alone the way. If we may prologise, this special law hopefully set up a quasi-judicial machinery for summary trial and speedy disposal and prescribed eviction save upon simple grounds safeguarding the security of tenants of buildings against being inequitably ejected.
L202: But this very case discloses the chronic distortion in processual justice, caused by a slow-motion spiral of appeals and plethora of technical pleas defeating the statutory design.
L203: 908
L204: The obvious legislative policy and project in this class of simplistic landlord-tenant litigation demands a radically non-traditional judicial structuring and legal engineering, by-passing sophistications and formalisms and tier-upon-tier of judicial reviews. Both these imperatives are conspicuously absent in current rent control litigation- a dismal failure which the legislature will, we hope, awaken to rectify.
L205: Post-audit of socio-economic laws in action, with a view to over-see if legal institutions and jural postulates actually achieve legislatively mandated objectives in special classes of dispute-proceessing, makes for competent and credible implementation of laws and saves the time of the higher courts and the money of the public at present consumed exasperatingly but avoidably. The price of legislative inaction in these areas is popular disenchantment with laws and tribunals.
L206: Factual matrix The appellant is the tenant of a building in Delhi having been inducted into possession by the respondent- landlord under a letting of May 19, 1954, evidenced by a deed which fixed the term merely as less than a year (a circumstance out of which a minor ripple of legal argument has arisen).
L207: At the time of the lease the cite12†Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (for short, the cite12†TP Act ), had not been extended to Delhi although, later, on December 1, 1962, the said Act was made applicable to this area. The landlord had been receiving rent from the tenant until the time he filed a petition for eviction (1967), the statute which regulated the right to eviction being the cite20†Delhi Rent Control Act, 1958 (59 of 1958) (for short, the cite19†Rent Act ).
L208: The eviction petition set out two grounds out of the many specified in cite21†s. 14 of the Rent Act, viz.,unauthorized sub-letting of a portion of the premises and possession, by the tenant, of alternative accommodation. Both these grounds having been made out, the evictibility under the cite19†Rent Act became inevitable.
L209: But, in the High Court, the appellant-tenant fell back on certain defences grounded on cite13†ss.106 and cite14†111 of the TP Act on the score that no notice to quit had been given, nor notice of forfeiture, as prescribed by those sections. There is no dispute that neither notice to quit nor notice of forfeiture determining the tenancy had been given by the landlord. The core of the controversy thus turns on the need to comply with the requirements of cite13†ss.
L210: 106 and/or 111 of the cite12†TP Act and the fatal effect of failure in this behalf. The landlord seeks to break through these defences by urging that the lease has expired by efflux of time limited thereby under s. 111(a) and no notice terminating the tenancy under cite13†s. 106 is needed and further that the forfeiture of the tenancy caused by sub-letting contrary to the terms of the deed of demise can be availed of by the landlord even in the absence of a notice as contemplated by cite16†s.
L211: 111 (g) because the cite12†TP Act , as amended by the cite22†Amending Act of 1929, did not, in terms, apply to the present lease and the principles of justice, equity and good conscience, which alone applied, did not desiderate the technical requirement of a notice in writing of an intention to determine the lease.
L212: The Rent Controller, at the floor level, ordered eviction and the Appellate Tribunal affirmed it, upholding the vice of sub-letting without consent of the landlord in the manner specified in cite23†s. 14(1)(b) as 909 also the disability spelt out in cite23†s. 14(1) (h) on acquiring vacant possession of alternative residence. The resistence founded on the cite12†TP Act was also over-ruled by the appellate Tribunal. But, when the case reached the High Court in second appeal, under cite24†s.
L213: 39 of the Rent Act, the learned Single Judge felt that certain points of law spun out of the cite12†TP Act deserved consideration by a Division Bench and referred the appeal for determination accordingly to a larger Bench. The Division Bench which heard the appeal dismissed it but granted a certificate of fitness for appeal to the Supreme Court under cite17†Art. 133 of the Constitution, restricting it, however, to but one ground urged before it. Shri A. K.
L214: Sen, for the appellant, made a gentle hint that the High Court had heard long arguments in March 1974 but could resolve its doubts to deliver a judgment only in May 1975 so much so the freshness of counsel's submissions might have faded somewhat and so we should have a closer look at his points de hors the judgment under appeal.
L215: If this fact of a long hiatus between hearing and decision were true, it must have inflicted a heavy strain on the memory of the learned Judges which it is a healthy practice to avoid. However, after listening to Shri A. K. Sen, we feel that his fears are unfounded.
L216: A preliminary pre-emptive objection was urged by the respondent that the High Court having circumscribed the certificate to a single point no other submissions should be permitted. We see no force in this untenable insistence on tying down the appellant. Once a certificate of fitness has been granted under cite17†Art. 133 , the appeal, in all its amplitude, is before this Court and every point may be urged by the appellant provided this Court permits it, having regard to the circumstances.
L217: Perhaps, a certificate under cite25†Art. 132 , or special leave under Art, 136 may stand on a different footing if the Court limits the grounds in any manner. Of course, conceding the Court's plenary power in appeals on certificate under cite17†Art. 133 , it is still within the Court's discretion not to allow a new point to be taken up [The rulings in 1963 (2) SCR 440 and 1964(2) SCR 930 lay down the law on this point].
L218: The contentions We have already indicated that, under the cite19†Rent Act two grounds for eviction have been good by the landlord. Indisputably, sub-letting has been substantiated. Even so, it is argued that only where a lease has been duly determined giving rise to a right to present possession under the cite12†TP Act can the landlord sue for recovery of the building.
L219: The scheme of the Rent Control law, speaking generally, is to put further fetters on landlords seeking eviction from urban buildings where, in the absence of such new barriers, they will be entitled to ejectment. The acute scracity of accommodation is the raison de'etre of the law. It is not as if the rent control statutes are a bonanza for the landlords and confer a relaxed right to eject where, under the general law, they do not have such a right in praesenti.
L220: To hold otherwise is to pervert the purpose and substitute an added danger for an extra dyke. It follows that even where under a particular rent control statute the landlord makes out grounds for eviction, he can institute proceedings in this behalf only if de hors the said grounds he has cause of action under the cite12†TP Act .
L221: 910
L222: We agree that, if the rent control legislation specifically provides grounds for eviction in supersession, not in supplementation, of what is contained in the cite12†T.P. Act , the situation may conceivably be different.
L223: cite19†But, in the Delhi Rent Act ,as in many other like Statutes, what is intended to be done is not to supplant but to supplement, not to eliminate the statutory requirements of determination of tenancy but to superimpose a ban on eviction which otherwise may be available in conformity with the cite12†TP Act without fulfilment of additional grounds. 'No order ..... for the recovery of possession of any premises shall be made...... in favour of the landlord against a tenant' is a blanket ban in cite21†s.
L224: 14(1) of the Rent Act. It is followed by enumeration of specific grounds proof of which may authorize the Controller to make an order for the recovery of possession of the premises. It follows that before a landlord can institute proceedings for recovery of possession, he has to make out his right (a) under the cite12†TP Act ; and (b) under the cite19†Rent Act .
L225: In Manujendra Dutt this Court considered the question elaborately and observed:
L226: > "cite26†The Thika Tenancy Act like similar Rent Acts passed in different States is intended to prevent indiscriminate eviction of tenants and is intended to be a protective statute to safeguard security of possession of tenants and therefore should be construed in the light of its being a social legislation.
L227: What cite27†section 3 therefore does is to provide that even where a landlord has terminated the contractual tenancy by a proper notice such landlord can succeed in evicting his tenant provided that he falls under one or more of the clauses of that section.
L228: The word 'notwithstanding' in cite27†section 3 on a true construction therefore means that even where the contractual tenancy is properly terminated, notwithstanding the landlord's right to possession under the cite12†Transfer of Property Act or the contract of lease he cannot evict the tenant unless he satisfied any one of the grounds set out in cite27†section 3 .
L229: Rent Acts are not ordinarily intended to interfere and with contractual leases and are Acts for the protection of tenants and are consequently restrictive and not enabling, conferring no new rights of action but restricting the existing rights either under the contract or under the general law.
L230: > * * * * * The right to hold over, that is, the right of irremovability thus is a right which comes into existence after the expiration of the lease and until the lease is terminated or expires by efflux of time the tenant need not seek protection under the cite19†Rent Act . For he is protected by his lease in breach of which he cannot be evicted. (See Maghji Lakshamshi and Bros v. Furniture Workshop-[1954] AC 80, 90). In Abasbhai v.
L231: Gulamnabi (AIR 1964 SC 1341), this Court clearly stated that the cite19†Rent Act did not give a right to the 911 landlord to evict a contractual tenant without first determining the contractual tenancy. In Mangilal v.
L232: Sugan Chand (AIR 1965 SC 101) while construing cite28†section 4 of the Madhya Pradesh Accommodation Control Act (XXIII of 1965), a section similar to cite27†section 3 of the present Act, this Court held that the provisions of cite29†section 4 of that Act were in addition to those of the cite12†Transfer of Property Act and therefore before a tenant could be evicted by a landlord, he must comply with both the provisions of cite13†section 106 of the Transfer of Property Act and those of cite29†section 4 .
L233: The Court further observed that notice under cite13†section 106 was essential to bring to an end the relationship of landlord and tenant and unless that relationship was validly terminated by giving a proper notice under cite13†s. 106 of the Transfer of Property Act, the landlord could not get the right to obtain possession of the premises by evicting the tenant (See also Haji Mohammad v. Rebati Bhushan-53 C.W.N. 859)."
L234: We are inclined to hold that the landlord in the present case cannot secure an order for eviction without first establishing that he has validly determined the lease under the cite12†TP Act .
L235: We are therefore thrown back to an examination of the argument pressed by the appellant-tenant that independently of the rent control law, the respondent has no subsisting cause of action. The contention is two-fold. Firstly, the lease is one where the time is not limited and therefore cite15†s. 111(a) will not apply and is terminable on the part of the lessor only in the manner provided by cite13†s. 106 , i.e., by 15 days' notice expiring with the end of the month of the tenancy.
L236: Admittedly, no such notice was given. The counter- contention of the landlord, apart from the plea of statutory tenancy requiring no further notice to determine, is that the lease is for a specified period even though it expresses itself as for a term less than one year and under cite15†s. 111 (a) has expired by efflux of time. We cannot agree to this feebly asserted argument.
L237: A lease merely stating that it is for a period less than one year is ex facie for an indefinite period and, as such, cannot expire by efflux of time. Nor are we convinced that, notwithstanding the acceptance of rent for the period of 11 years the landlord had not assented to the holding over of the tenancy and that what emerged was a statutory tenancy which did not require notice in law for valid determination. Possibly so; not necessarily.
L238: However, we need not explore this aspect further in the view that we take of the other submission of the landlord that the lease has been determined by forfeiture, not in terms of cite16†s. 111 (g) of the TP Act, but on the application of the principles of justice, equity and good conscience. We will examine this latter contention in some detail, as it is decisive of the fate of the case.
L239: cite19†The Rent Act contemplates no elaborate pleadings but filling out of particulars in a pro forma which takes the place of a plaint. No specific averment of forfeiture and consequent determination of the lease is found in the petition. Having regard to the comparative informality 912 of these proceedings and the quasi-judicial nature of the whole process, such an omission cannot be exaggerated into a lethal infirmity.
L240: What is perhaps more pertinent is that the petitioner was innocent of the plea of forfeiture throughout the stages of the trial before the Rent Controller. When the case reached the appellate stage, it was specifically urged that the tenancy 'stood terminated by forfeiture under cite16†s. 111 (g) of the TP Act.
L241: The Tribunal studied the terms of the rent deed, Exhibit AW 3/1 and held that there was an express condition against sub-letting and a provision that on breach thereof the lessor had the right to move for eviction- something equivalent to a right to re-enter.
L242: The tenant remonstrated against this new plea being permitted in appeal but the Court construed the statement in the pro-forma in column 18-B, that no notice is necessary, to mean that there was a determination by forfeiture even without the issuance of a notice. More over, the Court noticed the fact that the question was only one of law and should be permitted in the interests of justice.
L243: After some consideration of the issue the Tribunal reached the result 'that the tenancy stood determined by forfeiture and therefore no notice was required'. We need not tarry further on the tenability of this conclusion since the matter has been more fully examined at the High Court level.
L244: Arguments before us have proceeded on the footing that a sub-tenancy has been created and this amounts to a breach of condition with a provision for re-entry. The tribunal in appeal held that no notice was necessary since the lease was created prior to the extension of the cite12†TP Act to Delhi. Although there is some confusion in this order about the determination of the lease being under cite16†s. 111(g) or outside it, the thrust of the holding is found in these concluding words:
L245: > "However, as held by the Supreme Court in Narender, Lokmanya Lodhi v. Face to face with the issue of forfeiture under s. 111(g), the appellant presses the position that since admittedly no notice in writing, as laid down in the section, has been issued, the eviction proceeding can be shot down by that legal missile alone.
L254: Before the amending Act of 1929, all that was necessary for the lessor to determine the demise on forfeiture was to do 'some act showing his intention to determine the lease'. The rule of English law before the enactment of the Law of Property Act, 1925 appears to be that a suit for ejectment is equivalent to re-entry. It has been held in India that an act showing the lessor's intention to determine the lease can take the form of the institution of an action in ejectment.
L255: The statutory law, as it now stands, however is that the happening of any of the events specified in s. 111(g) does not, ipso facto, extinguish the lease but only exposes the lessee to the risk of forfeiture and clothes the lessor with the right, if he so chooses, to determine the lease, by giving notice in that behalf. Mulla states the law correctly thus:
L256: > "Forfeiture of a lease requires the operation of two factors: (1) A breach by the lessee of an express condition of the lease which provides for re-entry on such breach and (2) a notice by the lessor expressing his intention to determine the lease."
L257: 
L258: (Mulla on TP Act, p. 746-747, 6th Ed.) The notice has to be in writing. In Namdeo Lokman Lodhi(1) this Court laid down the law to the same effect. Mahajan J., observed:
L259: > "cite16†Section 111(g) in the terms makes the further act an integral condition of the forfeiture. In other words, without this act there is no completed forfeiture at all. Under the old section an overt act evidencing the requisite intention was 914 essential. As the law stands today, under the Act, notice in writing by the landlord is a condition precedent to a forfeiture and the right of re-entry."
L260: It cannot be gainsaid that a notice, as envisioned in cite16†s. 111(g) not having been given to the lessee in the present case, determination of the demise under cite16†s. 111(g) cannot be claimed by the lessor.
