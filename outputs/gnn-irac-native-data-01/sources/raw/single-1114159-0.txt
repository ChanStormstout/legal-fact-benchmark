L81:     HEADNOTE:
L82:          The  respondent  was  the	daughter  of  the   original
L83:     landlord  who had let out the premises to the  appellant  on
L84:     October 1, 1961.  She purchased the property from her father
L85:     on  June  27, 1964 and thus stepped into his  shoes  as	 the
L86:     `landlord' as defined under cite13†section 2 (e) of the Delhi	Rent
L87:     Control Act, 1958.
L88:          The  respondent sought eviction of the  appellant	from
L89:     the  demised  premises on the ground  of  personal  bonafide
L90:     requirement.   The appellant resisted the eviction  petition
L91:     on  the	 grounds  that the premises were  not  let  out	 for
L92:     residential  purpose  only but for commercial  purpose	also
L93:     i.e. for keeping foreign students as paying guests, and that
L94:     the respondent does not have a bonafide need or	 requirement
L95:     as such.
L96:          Relying upon the Rent Note and the appellant's  letters
L97:     dated  October 7, 1961 and August 18, 1962 addressed to	 the
L98:     respondent's  father,  and the earlier	proceedings  between
L99:     them  for  eviction of the appellant on the ground  of	sub-
L100:     letting	 the  premises	for  commercial	 purpose,  both	 the
L101:     statutory authorities-the Additional Rent Controller and the
L102:     Rent  Control  Tribunal found that the	premises  which	 had
L103:     also been used incidentally for commercial purposes so as to
L104:     exclude	 the application of cite12†section 14(1) (e) read with	 the
L105:     explanation   thereto,	 and  dismissed	  the	respondent's
L106:     application for eviction.

L108:          This  finding  was reversed by the High  Court  in	 the
L109:     respondent's second appeal under cite14†Section 29 of the Act.	 The
L110:     High  Court  found  that  there	 was  no  evidence  for	 the
L111:     statutory authorities to come to the conclusion, which	they
L112:     did, as regards the premises having been used for commercial
L113:     purpose.   The High Court accepted the appeal and set  aside
L114:     the  judgment and order of the Rent Controller and the	Rent
L115:     Control Tribunal, and allowed the eviction application.
L116:          The  tenant appealed to this Court by  Special  appeal.
L117:     On  behalf of the respondent-landlord it was submitted	that
L118:     even if the High Court was wrong in coming to the conclusion
L119:     that  there  was no evidence about  foreign  students  being
L120:     lodged	by the tenant, the mere fact that  foreign  students
L121:     stayed as paying guests in the premises did not imply either
L122:     that  they lodged with the consent of the landlord  or	that
L123:     such  lodging amounted to a commercial use of the  building,
L124:     and that the High Court was right in saying that the  ground
L125:     contained in clause (e) of sub-section (1) of cite15†section 14 was
L126:     attracted.
L127:          Allowing the appeal, and setting aside the judgment  of
L128:     the  High Court, and restoring the orders of the  Additional
L129:     Rent Controller and the Rent Control Tribunal, this Court,
L130:          HELD:   1.	  The	finding	 of  the   High	  Court	  is
L131:     unsustainable.	 The High Court was not justified in  saying
L132:     that  there was no evidence to hold that the  premises	were
L133:     used  for  boarding  and  lodging  foreign  students.	 The
L134:     specific plea of the landlord in the earlier proceedings was
L135:     that  the  tenant had sub-let the  premises  for  commercial
L136:     purposes.   The tenant contended that she had  never  parted
L137:     with  her exclusive possession of any part of  the  premises
L138:     and the foreign students who were lodging with her were	 her
L139:     paying	guests and were not her tenants.  The plea  of	sub-
L140:     tenancy	 raised	 by the landlord was thus  rejected  on	 the
L141:     ground	that those who lodged with her were not	 sub-tenants
L142:     but only paying guests. [476 G-H]
L143:          2.	 The  letters dated October 7, 1961 and	 August	 18,
L144:     1962  clearly disclosed the fact that foreign students	were
L145:     lodged in the premises as the guests of the appellant.	 The
L146:     evidence let in by the appellant and not contradicted by the
L147:     respondent clearly showed that apart from the appellant	 all
L148:     the  other  inmates of the premises  were  foreign  students
L149:     staying	 with  her  as her  paying  guests.   The  appellant
L150:     testified that she earned her livelihood from the income she
L151:     received  as lodging fee from students who lodged with	her,
L152:     and
L153:     						       474
L154:     that  is  was  out  of that income  that  all  her  personal
L155:     expenses including the rent payable by her for the  premises
L156:     had  been  met.	  These are the findings of  the  two  fact-
L157:     finding	 authorities, and those findings are based  on	oral
L158:     and  documentary evidence.  To have reversed those  findings
L159:     by  the High Court in Second Appeal on the ground that	they
L160:     were perverse was totally uncalled for. [477 A-C]
L161:          3. In the absence of any question of law, much less any
L162:     substantial  question  of  law,	 the  High  Court  was	 not
L163:     justified  in  reversing  the  concurrent  findings  of	 the
L164:     statutory authorities. [480 B]
L165:          4. Clause (e) of cite12†section 14(1) of the Act is applicable
L166:     only if the landlord is in a position to establish that	 the
L167:     premises let for residential purposes are required bona fide
L168:     by him for occupations as residence.  Assuming that the bona
L169:     fide requirement of the landlord is established the landlord
L170:     must  still  prove  that  the  premises	 had  been  let	 for
L171:     residential purposes. The Explanation of clause (e) makes it
L172:     clear that the words `premises let for residential purposes'
L173:     included any premises let for residential purposes but	used
L174:     incidentally,  without	the  consent of	 the  landlord,	 for
L175:     commercial or other purposes.  The Explanation is  attracted
L176:     when  :	 (1)  the premises have	 been  let  for	 residential
L177:     purposes, (ii) the premises have been used incidentally	 for
L178:     commercial or other purposes,and (iii) the landlord has	 not
L179:     given his consent for such incidental use for commercial  or
L180:     other purpose. [478 D-F]
L181:          5.	 If the premises have never been used for  any	non-
L182:     residential   purpose,	the  aid  of  the   explanation	  is
L183:     unnecessary  to	 attract  clause (e).	The  Explanation  is
L184:     called	in  aid	 only where  premises  let  for	 residential
L185:     purpose have been used incidentally for commercial or  other
L186:     non-residential	 purpose,  but without the  consent  of	 the
L187:     landlord. [478 G]
L188:          6.	 If the landlord is in a position to establish	that
L189:     the premises have been let for residential purposes and that
L190:     he  has never consented to the user of the premises for	 any
L191:     other  purpose, the mere fact that such premises  have	been
L192:     incidentally used for commercial or other purposes would not
L193:     change or affect the residential character of the  premises.
L194:     [479 A]
L195:          7.	 If the premises have been regularly and openly used
L196:     for  non-residential purposes, the knowledge and consent  of
L197:     the landlord, unless proved to the contrary, are  ordinarily
L198:     presumed and in
L199:     						       475
L200:     that event the explanation would be of no avail to save	 the
L201:     ground under clause (e). [479 B]
L202:          8.	 In  the instant case, it is not disputed  that	 the
L203:     premises  had been let for residential purposes, but  it  is
L204:     also beyond doubt that to the knowledge of the landlord	 the
L205:     premises have been regularly used by the tenant not only for
L206:     her  own  residence but also for her  foreign  guests.	 The
L207:     landlord  has as all material times known or is presumed  to
L208:     have known that foreign students have been staying with	 the
L209:     appellant  as her paying  guests and that she has been	ever
L210:     since 1961 running a boarding house in the premises.  At  no
L211:     time  did   the landlord object to the user of the  premises
L212:     by the appellant for such purpose. [479 C-D]
L213:          9.	 The continued user of the building ever since	1961
L214:     for  the  purpose of lodging paying guests  shows  that	 the
L215:     respondent-landlord and her father have not only been  aware
L216:     of  such  user	of the building,  but  have  also  impliedly
L217:     consented  to such user.  This presumption  is	irresistible

L219:     in   question  out  of	the  ambit  of	`premises  let	 for

L221:     in clause (e). [479 E]

L231:     JUDGMENT:
L232: CIVIL APPELLATE JURISDICTION: Civil Appeal No. 1993 of 1982.
L233: 
L234: From the Judgment and Order dated 9.2.1982 of the Delhi High Court in S.A.O No. 59 of 1979.
L235: 
L236: M.K.Ramamurthi, Mrs. Chandan Ramamurthi and M.A.Krishnamoorthi for the Appellants.
L237: Harish N. Salve and A.K.Sanghi for the Respondents. The Judgment of the Court was delivered by T.K.THOMMEN, J. This appeal arises from the judgment of the 476 Delhi High Court in S.A.O.No. 59 of 1979 whereby the High Court, reversing the concurrent findings of the Additional Rent Controller and the Rent Control Tribunal, allowed the respondent-landlord's application for eviction of the appellant-tenant under cite12†section 14(1)(e) of the Delhi Rent Control Act, 1958 (the `Act').
L238: The respondent is the daughter of the original landlord who had let out the premises to the appellant on 1.10.1961. The present respondent purchased the property from her father on 27th June, 1964 and thus stepped into his shoes as the `landlord' as defined under cite13†section 2(e) of the Act.
L239: Relying upon the Rent Note and the appellant's letters dated 7.10.1961 and 18.8.1962 addressed to the respondent's father and the earlier proceedings between them for eviction of the appellant on the ground of sub-letting the premises for commercial purposes, both the statutory authorities found that the premises which had been let out for residential purposes to the appellant had also been used incidentally for commercial purposes so as to exclude the application of cite12†section 14 (1) (e) read with the Explanation thereto.
L240: This finding was reversed by the High Court by the impugned judgment. This High Court found that there was no evidence for the statutory authorities to come to the conclusion, which they did, as regards the premises having been used for commercial purpose. This is what the High Court says:-
L241: > "........No documentary evidence has been brought on record to hold that the premises were ever used for boarding and lodging foreign students.....Thus there is no evidence on record to hold that the premises were used for boarding and lodging of the foreign students or that the premises were let to the respondent for commercial purposes.
L242: Thus, I am of the view that the premises were let to the respondent for use as residence and the findings to contrary by the controller and the Tribunal are without any evidence on record and are perverse".
L243: This finding of the High Court is, in our view, unsustainable. The High Court was not justified in saying that there was no evidence to hold that the premises were used for boarding and lodging foreign students. The specific plea of the landlord in the earlier proceedings was that the tenant had sub-let the premises for commercial purposes.
L244: The tenant contended that she had never parted with her exclusive possession of any part of the premises and the foreign students who were lodging were her paying guests and were not her tenants. The plea of sub-tenancy raised by the landlord was thus rejected on the ground that those who logged with her were not sub-tenants but only paying guests.
L245: Letters dated 7.10.1961 477 and 18.8.1962 addressed by the appellant-tenant to the respondent-landlord were considered by the authorities in coming to the conclusion, which they did. These letters clearly disclosed the fact that foreign students were lodged in the evidence let in by the appellant and not contradicted by the respondent clearly showed that apart from the appellant, all the other inmates of the premises were foreign students staying with her as her paying guests.
L246: The appellant testified to the effect that she earned her livelihood from the income she received as lodging fee from students who lodged with her. It was out of that income that all her personal expenses including the rent payable by her for the premises had been met. These are the findings of the two fact-finding authorities and those findings are based on oral and documentary evidence.
L247: To have reversed those findings by the High Court in Second Appeal on the ground that they were perverse was totally uncalled for.
L248: Mr Harish Salve appearing for the respondent-landlord submits that even if the High Court was wrong in coming to the conclusion that there was no evidence about foreign students stayed as paying guests in the premises did not imply either that they lodged with the consent of the landlord or that such lodging amounted to a commercial use of the building.
L249: Counsel submits that the High Court was right on the facts of this case in saying that the ground contained in clause (e) of section (1) of cite15†section 14 was attracted.
L250: There is no substance in the contention that the landlord was unaware that the premises had been used for lodging foreign students. The two letters relied on by the statutory authorities leave no doubt that this fact was well-known to the landlord at all material times. To the knowledge of the landlord the premises have been regularly used by the tenant ever since 1961 for the residence of not only herself but also of the foreign students who were lodged by her for gain as paying guests.
L251: The evidence is that she had no income other than what she received as lodging fee from foreign students. The question then is whether the facts found excluded the application of the ground contained in clause (e) of the cite12†section 14 (1) .
L252: cite15†Section 14 , insofar as it is material, reads:
L253: 
L254: > "cite15†S.14 . Protection of tenant against eviction:- (1) Notwithstanding anything to the contrary contained in any other law or contract, no order or decree for the recovery of possession of any premises shall be made by any court or Controller in favour of the landlord against a tenant:
L255: 
L256: 478
L257: > Provided that the Controller may, on an application made to him in the prescribed manner, make an order for the recovery of possession of the premises on one or more of the following grounds only, namely-
L258: > (e) that the premises let for residential purpose are required bona fide by the landlord for occupation as a residence for himself or for any member of his family dependent on him, if he is the owner thereof, or for any person for whose benefit the premises are held and that the landlord or such person has no other reasonably suitable residential accommodation;
L259: Explanation-For the purposes of this clause, "premises let for residential purposes" include any premises which having been let for use as a residence are, without the consent of the landlord, used incidentally for commercial or other purposes".
L260: The only ground which is relied on by the landlord is that which is contained in clause(e) read with the Explanation. Clause (e) on the facts alleged is applicable only if the landlord is in a position to establish that the premises let for residential purposes are required bona fide by him for occupation as a residence. Assuming that the bona fide requirement of the landlord is established, the landlord must still prove that the premises had been let for residential purposes.
L261: The Explanation to clause (e) makes it clear that the words `premises let for residential purposes' include any premises let for residential purposes, but used incidentally, without the consent of the landlord, for commercial or other purposes.
L262: The Explanation is attracted when (i) the premises have been let for residential purposes, (ii) the premises have been used incidentally for commercial or other purposes, and (iii) the landlord had not given his consent for such incidental use for commercial or other purposes. If the three ingredients contained in the Explanation are attracted, the premises do not cease to be "Premises let for residential purposes"
L263: > falling under clause (e). In respect of such premises, the bona fide requirement of the landlord referred to in clause
L264: 
L265: > (e) is a ground for eviction.
L266: If the premises have never been used for any non- residential purpose, the aid of the Explanation is unnecessary to attract clause (e). The Explanation is called in aid only where premises let for residential purposes have been used incidentally for commercial or other non-residential purposes, but without the consent of the landlord. The fundamental question in respect of residential premises is whether the landlord had consented to the user of the premises for any other purpose, albeit incidentally.
L267: 479
L268: If the landlord is in a position to establish that the premises have been let for residential purposes and that he has never consented to the user of the premises for any other purpose, the mere fact that such premises have been incidentally used for commercial or other purposes would not change or affect the residential character of the premises. In respect of such premises, it is open to the landlord to prove his bona fide requirements and thus establish the ground mentioned under clause (e).
L269: On the other hand, if the premises have been regularly and openly used for non- residential purposes, the knowledge and constant of the landlord, unless proved to the contrary, are ordinarily presumed and in that event the Explanation would be of no avail to save the ground under clause (e).
L270: In the present case, it is not disputed that the premises had been let for residential purposes, but it is also beyond doubt that to the knowledge of the landlord the premises have been regularly used by the tenant not only for her own residence but also for her foreign guests.
L271: The landlord has at all material times known or is presumed to have known that foreign students have been staying with the appellant as her paying guests and that she has been ever since 1961 running a boarding house in the premises. At no time did the landlord object to the user of the premises by the appellant for such purpose.
L272: The continued user of the building ever since 1961 for the purpose of lodging paying guests shows that the respondent-landlord and her father have not only been aware of such user of the building, but have also impliedly consented to such user. This presumption is irresistible from the evidence on record. Such user takes the premises in question out of the ambit of `premises let for residential purposes' so as to exclude the ground contained in clause (e).
L273: We are fortified in our conclusion by the views expressed by this Court in cite16†Dr. Gopal Dass Verma v. Dr. S.K. Bhardwaj & Anr. , [1962] 2 SCR 678 and Kartar Singh v. Chaman Lal & Ors., SC (1969) IV All India Rent Control Journal 349.
L274: The position would have been probably different, and the Explanation would have been still available, had foreign guests been lodged only occasionally and for short periods, even if it be on the basis of payment to cover expenses. All this is a question of intention. Was it an occasional accommodation of paying guests consistently with the character of the premises as a private residence?
L275: The evidence on record leaves no doubt that the premises have been regularly used by the appellant as a boarding house and not as a private 480 residence in the ordinary acceptation of the term. She has in fact been carrying on, in the words of Romer, J., "a species of business". See Hobson v. Tulloch [1898] 1 Chancery Division 424. See also Thorn & Ors. v. Madden [1925] All E.R. 321 and Tendler v. Sproule [1947] 1 All E.R.
L276: 193. In the absence of any question of law, much less any substantial question of law, the High Court was not justified in reversing the concurrent findings of the statutory authorities.
L277: In the circumstances, we set aside the impugned judgment of the High Court and restore the orders of the Additional Rent Controller dated 29.9.1976 and Rent Control Tribunal dated 18.11.1978. The appeal is allowed in the above terms with the costs of the appellant throughout.
