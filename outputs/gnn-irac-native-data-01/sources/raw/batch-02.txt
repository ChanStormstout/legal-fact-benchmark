L37: ## Pooran Chand vs Motilal And Ors. on 11 December, 1962
L38: 
L39: ### Equivalent citations: AIR1964SC461, [1963]SUPP2SCR906
L40: ### Bench: cite8†J.L. Kapur , cite9†J.R. Mudholkar , cite10†K. Subba Rao , cite11†S.J. Imam L41: 
L42:     JUDGMENT
L43: 
L44: 
L45:      Subba Rao, J.
L46: 
L47:

L50: 1. This appeal by special leave is directed against the judgment and decree of the High Court of Judicature for Rajasthan at Jodhpur setting aside those of the Senior Civil Judge, Ajmer, and restoring those of the Subordinate Judge, First Class, Ajmer, decreeing the suit for eviction from the suit premises filed by the respondents against the appellant.
L51: 2. The facts may be briefly stated. The building situate at No. 41 Purani Mandi, Ajmer, consists of a large number of rooms, and the respondents are its owners. On October 13, 1935, the said building was taken on lease by the appellant's father for a period of one year on a rent of Rs. 50/- per month. On July 10, 1950, the respondents gave a lease of the said building in favour of the appellant for a period of one year on a rent of Rs. 65/- per month.
L52: On August 8, 1952, a fresh lease was executed in favour of the appellant on an enhanced rent of Rs. 70/- per month. Under the said lease the tenancy was to commence from August 1, 1952. On June 27, 1954, the respondents issued a notice to the appellant, through their Advocates, calling upon him to vacate the premises by midnight of July 31, 1954/August 1, 1954. In that notice it was alleged that the appellant was in arrears of rent and that he had also sublet the property.
L53: In the reply notice the appellant promised to pay the arrears of rent as early as possible, but stated that he had all along been subletting portions of the premises to others, except the portion under his occupation. As the appellant did not comply with the terms of the notice, the respondents filed on August 2, 1954, Civil Suit No. 762 of 1954 in the Court of the Subordinate Judge, First Class, Ajmer, against the appellant for eviction, for recovery of arrears of rent and for other reliefs.
L54: The plaint was later on amended. The appellant contested the suit on various grounds and particularly on the ground that it was not maintainable. It may be mentioned that in the written-statement the fact that the premises were sublet to tenants was not denied. The learned Subordinate Judge decreed the suit, holding that the notice was valid and that the appellant was liable to be evicted under cite12†s.
L55: 13(1)(b) of the Delhi and Ajmer Rent Control Act, 1952, (XXXVIII of 1952), hereinafter called the Act, as he had sublet portions of the premises without the consent in writing of the landlord. On appeal the Senior Civil Judge, Ajmer, allowed the appeal. He held that the notice issued to the appellant was short by 24 hours and that he had no right to sublet the premises without the written consent of the landlord, though there where sub-tenants in the premises when the appellant took the lease.
L56: On second appeal, the High Court allowed the appeal and restored the decree of the trial court. The High Court held that the notice complied with the provisions of cite13†s. 106 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, and that, in any event, as the tenancy expired by mere efflux of time, no notice was necessary. Hence the present appeal.
L57: 3. Learned counsel for the appellant raised before us the following four points : (1) No second appeal lay to the High Court against the decree and judgment of the Civil Judge; (2) if no second appeal lay against the decree and judgment of the Civil Judge, the High Court's power of interference with that judgment was confined only to cite14†s.
L58: 35 of the Act and that under that section it had no jurisdiction to set aside the judgment on merits, whether of law or of fact; (3) the High Court wrongly held that the notice complied with the provisions of cite13†s. 106 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882; and (4) the High Court made out a totally new case in holding that the tenancy had expired by efflux of time.
L59: 4. It is not necessary in this case to express our opinion on the first question, as we are satisfied that even if no second appeal lay to the High Court against the judgment and decree of the Civil Judge, the High Court had ample jurisdiction to interfere in the circumstances of the case under cite15†s. 35(1) of the Act, which reads :
L60: > "(1) The High Court may, at any time, call for the record of any case under this Act for the purpose of satisfying itself that a decision made therein is according to law and may pass such order in relation thereto as it thinks fit."
L61: 5. Reliance is placed by the learned counsel on a decision of this Court in cite16†Hari Shankar v. Rao Girdhari Lal Chowdhury [[1962] Supp. 1 S.C.R. 933], in support of the contention that the jurisdiction of the High Court under cite15†s. 35 of the Act is very limited and does not warrant the High Court's interference in the circumstances of this case.
L62: The main question in that decision was whether the plaintiff consented to the subletting of parts of the demised premises and if so, when and to what effect ? The trial Judge found that there was no evidence that the landlord was ever consulted. On appeal the District Judge confirmed that finding. In revision, the High Court considered the evidence over again and came to a contrary conclusion. In that context this Court considered the scope of cite15†s. 35 of the Act.
L63: Hidayatullah, J., expressing the majority view, observed :
L64: > "The phrase "according to law" refers to the decision as a whole, and is not to be equated to errors of law or of fact simpliciter. It refers to the overall decision, which must be according to law which it would not be, if there is a miscarriage of justice due to a mistake of law. The section is thus framed to confer larger powers than the power to correct error of jurisdiction to which cite17†s. 115 (cite18†of the Code of Civil Procedure ) is limited."
L65: 6. Then the learned Judge quoted in extenso the observations of Beaumont, C.J. (as he then was) in cite19†Bell & Co. Ltd. v. Waman Hemraj [(1938) 40 Bom. L.R. 125] and recorded his full concurrence with those observations. By those observations the learned Chief Justice gave certain illustrations and made it clear that they were not exhaustive and concluded thus :
L66: > "But, in my opinion, the Court ought not to interfere merely because it thinks that possibly the Judge who heard the case may have arrived at a conclusion which the High Court would not have arrived at."
L67: 7. It is clear from the observations of Hidyatullah, J., and those of Beaumont, C.J., which the former has fully extracted, that the power of the High Court under cite15†s. 35 of the Act is wider than that under cite20†s. 115 of the Code of Civil Procedure, though it cannot be equated to that of its jurisdiction in an appeal. It is neither possible not advisable to define with precision the scope and ambit of cite15†s.
L68: 35 of the Act, but it should be left to the High Court to consider in each case whether the impugned judgment is according to law or not, as explained by this Court in cite19†the said decision .
L69: 8. Bearing the view expressed by this Court in mind we shall proceed to consider whether the High Court had acted within its jurisdiction. The main question turns upon the construction of cite21†s. 13(1) of the Act. The material part of the section reads :
L70: > "Notwithstanding anything to the contrary contained in any other law or any contract, no decree or order for the recovery of possession of any premises shall be passed by any Court in favour of the landlord against any tenant (including a tenant whose tenancy is terminated) :
L71: 
L72: > Provided that nothing in this sub-section shall apply to any suit or other proceeding for such recovery of possession if the Court is satisfied -
L73: > (a) that the tenant has neither paid nor tendered the whole of the arrears of rent due within one month of the date on which a notice of demand for the arrears of rent has been served on him by the landlord in the manner provided in section 106 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (IV of 1882); or
L74: 
L75: > (b) that the tenant without obtaining the consent of the landlord in writing has, after the commencement of this Act, -
L76: > (i) sub-let, assigned or otherwise parted with the possession of, the whole or any part of the premises."..........
L77: 9. Learned counsel for the appellant contends that the provisions of the said section are an additional protection to a tenant and that they do not enable the landlord to dispense with a statutory notice before filing a suit for eviction, and in the present case the notice given did not comply with the provisions of cite13†s. 106 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882.
L78: It is not necessary in this appeal to express our opinion on the validity of this contention, for we are satisfied that the term of the tenancy had expired by efflux of time; and, therefore, no question of statutory notice would arise.
L79: But the learned counsel contends that this point was not raised either in the plaint or in the lower courts, but was raised for the first time before the High Court and that as the question is a mixed question of fact and law, the High Court went wrong in allowing it to be raised for the first time before it. We cannot say that this point was not raised in the plaint.
L80: The suit was filed for eviction, and the ground for eviction was two-fold, viz., the rent was not paid and that the appellant had sublet the premises. In the plaint it was not stated that the tenancy was a monthly tenancy; on the other hand, the respondents alleged in the plaint that the appellant was their tenant under the lease deed dated August 8, 1952, and they filed, along with the plaint the said lease deed, the terms whereof clearly show that the term of the was for one year.
L81: The appellant admitted those facts. It is, therefore, manifest that the appellant never denied that the term of the lease was not for one year. The High Court was, therefore, justified in considering the point, because the validity of the notice depended upon the term of the tenancy and also because the question of the term of the tenancy depended solely on the construction of the lease deed.
L82: On the basis of the lease deed the High Court held that the term of the lease is only for one year and it had expired by efflux of time.
L83: The document says that the house had been taken on rent for one year by the first party and ends thus, "if the rent falls into arrears then the second party shall be jointly and severally entitled to eject me namely the first party before the expiry of the term of tenancy and realise the rent due." It is, therefore, manifest that the lease was for a period of one year and that it was not a monthly tenancy.
L84: As the term fixed under the deed had expired, the appellant was not entitled to any statutory notice under cite13†s. 106 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882.
L85: 10. Even so, it is contended that the appellant had not sublet the premises within the meaning of cite21†s. 13(1)(b)(i) of the Act. It is said that the sub-section applies only to a case of sub-tenancy created for the first time after the lease was taken and does not cover a case where there was already a sub-tenant and a new subtenant was inducted when the previous sub-tenant vacated it.
L86: This conclusion is sought to be drawn from the words "sublet, assigned, or otherwise parted with the possession" and it is argued if possession had already been parted with by way of sub-lease and what was done was only to substitute another in the place of the earlier sub-tenant, this sub-clause is not attracted. There are no merits in this contention.
L87: cite21†Section 13(1)(b)(i) clearly says that if a tenant, without obtaining the consent of the landlord in writing has, after the commencement of this Act, sub-let, assigned or otherwise parted with the possession of the whole or any part of the premises, he is liable to be evicted. Here, admittedly after the lease deed of 1952 the appellant has sublet some of the rooms of the building to others without obtaining the written consent of the landlord.
L88: The fact that there were sub-tenants in the said portions could not conceivably be of any help to the appellant, because the new sub-tenants were not holding under the earlier sub-tenants, but were inducted by the appellant, after the earlier sub-tenancies were terminated. The appellant, having sub-let part of the premises without the consent of the landlord in writing, cannot invoke the protection given to him under cite21†s. 13 of the Act.
L89: 11. In this view, the High Court was certainly right in setting aside the decree of the Civil Judge, for the Civil Judge refused to pass an order of eviction on a wrong legal basis that the appellant was a monthly tenant, ignoring the express term in the lease deed itself. As the decree was not "according to law", the High Court, in exercise of its jurisdiction under cite15†s. 35 of the Act, was certainly within its rights to set aside the said decree.
L90: 12. In the result, the appeal fails and is dismissed with costs.
L91: 
L92: 13. Appeal dismissed.

L81:     HEADNOTE:
L82:     By cite15†s. 13 of the Delhi and Ajmer Rent Control Act, 1952 which
L83:     came into force on June 9, 1952, courts were prohibited from
L84:     directing  eviction  of a tenant at the suit of	 a  landlord
L85:     excepting  in  the  cases mentioned in the  proviso  to	 it.
L86:     Clause	(c)  of the proviso permitted  ejectment  where	 the
L87:     "tenant without obtaining the consent of landlord has before
L88:     the  commencement of this Act sub-let. . . .  the  premises"
L89:     Relying on this clause the respondent landlord filed a	suit
L90:     against	 the  appellant	 and  respondent  no.  2  for  their
L91:     ejectment  from a shop room let to the latter alleging	that
L92:     it  had been sub-let to the appellant without  his  consent.
L93:     The  appellant	resisted  the suit on the  ground  that	 the
L94:     respondent land lord had acquiesced in the subletting.	 The
L95:     trial  judge  decreed the suit holding that  the  respondent
L96:     landlord  had not done so.  The appellant alone appealed  to
L97:     the  Additional Senior Sub-judge who set aside the order  of
L98:     the trial judge taking the view that the respondent landlord
L99:     had  acquiesced in the sub-letting.  He also held  that	 the
L100:     subletting had commenced not later than November 1950.	 The
L101:     landlord moved the High Court in revision under cite16†s. 35 of the
L102:     Act.
L103:     While  the matter was pending in the cite12†High Court, the Delhi Rent Control Act, 1958 , came into force.  cite13†Section 57 of	 the
L104:     Act of 1958, provided; "(1) cite14†The Delhi and Ajmer Rent Control Act, 1952 ,  in	 so far as it is  applicable  to  the  Union
L105:     Territory of Delhi, is hereby repealed. (2)  Notwithstanding
L106:     such repeal, all suits and other proceedings under the	said
L107:     Act  pending,  at the commencement of this Act,	 before	 any
L108:     court or other authority shall be continued and disposed  of
L109:     in accordance with the provisions of the said Act, as if the
L110:     said  Act had continued in force and this Act had  not	been
L111:     passed: Provided that in any such suit or proceeding for the
L112:     fixation  of standard rent or for the eviction of  a  tenant
L113:     from any premises to which s. 54does not apply, the court or
L114:     other authority shall have regard to the provisions of	this
L115:     Act:  Provided	further that the, provisions for  under	 the
L116:     said  Act shall continue in force in respect of	 Suit,,	 and
L117:     proceedings disposed of thereunder."
L118:     The Court held that by reason of the provisions of subs. (2)
L119:     of cite14†S. 57 of the Act of 1958 the revision case had to be dis-
L120:     posed  of  in accordance with the provisions of the  Act  of
L121:     1952.	It also held that there was no evidence	 to  justify
L122:     the  appellate	court's	 findings that	the  respondent	 had
L123:     acquiesced  in	the sub-letting by respondent no. 2  to	 the
L124:     appellant.   In	 that  view of the  matter  the	 High  Court
L125:     allowed	 the  petition	of  revision.	The  appellant	then
L126:     appealed to this Court.
L127:     Held:	  It was competent for the High Court under cite16†s. 35 of
L128:     the Act of 1952 to interfere with the findings of the  court
L129:     below  on  the question of acquiescene on  the	ground	that
L130:     there  was no evidence to support that finding.	 If a  court
L131:     had arrived at a finding without any evidence to support it,
L132:     it can be legitimately said that it had not decided the case
L133:     "according to law" within the meaning of that expression  in cite16†s. 35 .
L134:     649
L135:     Hari Shankar v. Rao Girdhai  Lall Chowdhury, 119621 Supp.  I
L136:     S.C.R.	933, Pooran Chand v. Motilal, 119631 Supp. 2  S.C.R.
L137:     906 and Lala Beni Ram v. Kundan Lal, (1899) L.R. 26 I.A. 58,
L138:     referred to.
L139:     The  right of' the appellant to challenge the decree of	 the
L140:     trial  Judge by appeal could not be affected by the  failure
L141:     of the respondent no. 2 to file an appeal.
L142:     Per  Das,  Acting  C. J., and Hidaytullah,  J.	(Sarkar,  J.
L143:     dissenting): The first proviso to cite17†s. 57(2) of the Delhi Rent
L144:     Control	 Act,  1958  does not demand that  a  suit  for	 the
L145:     eviction  of it tenant filed under the Delhl and Ajmer	Rent
L146:     Control	 Act,  1952,  must  be	governed  entirely  by	 the
L147:     provisions  of	the  new  Act.	 The  provisions  applicable
L148:     continue  to  be  the provisions of the old  Act  with	this
L149:     addition  that	where the new Act has slightly	modified  or
L150:     clarified  the previous provisions, those modifications	 and
L151:     clarifications should IV applied.  Where entirely new rights
L152:     and  new liabilities have been created, the  new  provisions
L153:     must  not be allowed to override the provisions of  the	 old
L154:     Act.  If the expression "shall have regard to the provisions
L155:     of this Act" in the first proviso to cite17†s. 57(2) means that the
L156:     provisions of the cite12†Delhi Rent Control Act, 1958 , shall  apply
L157:     to  ill such suits or proceedings as are referred to  in  s.
L158:     57(2) except in the matter of the jurisdiction of the  civil
L159:     court, then in reality the substantive provision of cite17†s. 57(2) L160:     will  be  denuded  of  its full	 effect	 for  all  practical
L161:     purposes.   Moreover,  that would be giving  effect  to	 the
L162:     provisions  of the cite18†Rent Control Act of 1958  retrospectively
L163:     though cite17†s. 57(2) states in clear terms that all  suits	 and
L164:     proceedings pending at the commencement of the new Act	will
L165:     be dealt with in accordance with provisions of the old	Act.
L166:     The   correct  approach	 is  to	 read  the   first   proviso
L167:     harmoniusly  with the substantive provision contained in  s.
L168:     57(2).
L169:     Per  Sarkar,  J. The expression "shall have  regard  to	 the
L170:     provisions  of the new Act" in cite17†s. 57(2) of the Act  of	1958
L171:     gives	to  all	 the  provisions  of  the  Act	of  1958   a
L172:     retrospective	operation   and	 not  to,  some	  of   those
L173:     provisions.
L174:     Those words do not mean that the intention was that some  of
