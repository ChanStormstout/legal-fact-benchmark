## Opposition and unresolved limits

The Mimanis' strongest substantive argument was that the State had taken possession from them while their lease remained valid, so ordinary principles of restoration required returning the property to the same persons. The Supreme Court acknowledged the initial plausibility of that principle but held that the intervening expiry of the lease changed the entitlement analysis. The relevant question was who possessed a subsisting right when restoration was ordered, not merely who had possessed the property in November 1987. Sources: IK-840688:L104, IK-840688:L124-L130, IK-840688:L185-L187.

Protection of a former tenant against wrongful dispossession was an accepted legal proposition, including the requirement that even a trespasser in possession cannot simply be evicted without due process. The decisive distinction was factual and remedial: the Mimanis were no longer in possession because the State had requisitioned the property, and the owners had not forcibly dispossessed them. The Court therefore distinguished a right protecting existing possession from an affirmative entitlement to regain possession after the legal basis for occupation had expired. Sources: IK-840688:L62-L70, IK-840688:L157, IK-840688:L163, IK-840688:L183-L186.

The Mimanis could argue that requisition was only temporary and did not permanently extinguish their possessory position. The Court nevertheless distinguished temporary State control from ownership, found that the Daw owners retained title, and treated the combination of possession passing to the State and expiry of the lease during that interval as fatal to an automatic return of possession to the former lessees. This was not framed as a rule that every requisition eliminates every lessee's interest. Sources: IK-840688:L87, IK-840688:L127-L130, IK-840688:L184-L186.

The Mimanis' procedural objection had substantial force. The first High Court judgment was final, and 'liberty to mention' could be used for clarification rather than reopening matters already decided. The Supreme Court expressly considered the second High Court judgment unwarranted on that procedural basis. It nevertheless examined entitlement independently under Article 136 because the merits had been extensively argued and time had elapsed. Consequently, procedural criticism of the second judgment did not become a substantive holding that possession must be delivered to the Mimanis. Sources: IK-840688:L136-L143, IK-840688:L158-L162.

The High Court's second judgment had reasoned that the Mimanis could not obtain relief because their appeal was no longer maintainable and their legal interest had expired. The Supreme Court acknowledged that criticism of these observations was justified and did not simply adopt the High Court's maintainability reasoning. It proceeded to decide whether any substantive entitlement survived, applying the lease-expiry and possession distinctions. Sources: IK-840688:L152-L162, IK-840688:L183-L187.

The respondents relied on Section 6 of the 1948 requisition statute, while the Mimanis invoked Section 7(3) concerning compensation for persons interested in the land. Neither submission could operate as a direct statutory source of restoration entitlement because the Court held the temporary statute had expired and its provisions could not be invoked. The Court's later discussion of Section 6 was expressly alternative: even its wording contemplated inquiry into present entitlement rather than an unconditional return to the prior possessor. Sources: IK-840688:L131-L135, IK-840688:L189-L192.

The possibility of post-expiry tenancy status did not preserve the Mimanis' restoration claim. The cited authority distinguished a tenant at sufferance, who remains in possession without consent, from a tenant holding over, whose continued possession has the landlord's assent. Both analyses presupposed possession continuing after expiry. The Court found that the Mimanis had not remained in possession and therefore qualified as neither category. Sources: IK-840688:L193-L203.

The Court also considered the obligation of a lessee to return possession upon determination of the lease and its own duty to do justice between the parties. Ordering restoration to the Mimanis after expiry of their lease, despite their absence from possession, would not follow merely from their earlier protection against forcible eviction. The source's inconsistent references to Section 108A and Section 108 Q should be preserved rather than silently corrected. Sources: IK-840688:L172-L182, IK-840688:L185-L188.

The alleged oral purchase agreement offered a potentially independent basis for later proceedings but did not establish immediate possession entitlement in the appeal. The specific performance suit's procedural history was recorded without adjudication of the agreement's merits, and the Court expressly preserved the parties' rights to pursue appropriate proceedings. This reservation qualified the reach of the dismissal without changing the conclusion about immediate restoration under the expired lease. Sources: IK-840688:L204-L208.
