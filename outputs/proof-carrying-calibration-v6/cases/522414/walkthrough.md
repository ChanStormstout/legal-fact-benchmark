## Opposition and unresolved limits

The appellants' strongest textual objection was that both the final decree for sale and the sale certificate expressly listed plot 160 and omitted plot 1060. Their position was that judicial recovery of an unlisted plot could not rest on an assumed correction. The Court accepted the literal discrepancy but rejected the inference that plot 1060 was absent from the property actually identified by those documents. The matching khata number, area, and boundaries supported identification independently of the mistaken number. References: IK-522414:L123-L127, IK-522414:L139-L145.

The appellants relied on Rambhadra Naidu v. Kadiriyasami Naicker for the importance and authority of a sale certificate as a document of title. The Supreme Court distinguished that decision because it concerned an attempt to contradict the property plainly included in the sale certificate by referring back to the mortgage deed. The respondents' position in the present case was that they were identifying the property described in the decree and certificate, not enlarging the sale beyond them. References: IK-522414:L127-L133.

A genuine change in the identity of the property could not be excused as a clerical irregularity. The judgment's account of Thakar Barmha v. Jiban Ram Marwari confirms that only the attached property could be sold and that substituting a different six-annas share was an identity issue. The distinguishing condition in the present case was the Court's finding that the land was already sufficiently identified by its other descriptors. References: IK-522414:L136-L139, IK-522414:L144-L145.

The presence of plot number 160 in both original sale documents raised a real identification objection, but the Court treated the absence of any plot 160 in khata 97 as additional evidence that the entry was a missing-zero error rather than an identification of another parcel within that khata. The judgment does not decide whether a plot 160 existed in any other khata. References: IK-522414:L124-L127, IK-522414:L139-L140.

The writ of delivery of possession and the respondents' subsequent sale deed identified plot 1060, supporting continuity between the judicial sale, delivery, and transfer. These documents alone were not treated as authority to convey additional land not sold in execution. Their role was corroborative, while the decisive identification rested on the matching particulars in the final decree and sale certificate themselves. References: IK-522414:L125-L126, IK-522414:L133, IK-522414:L139-L145.

The Court expressly recognized a limiting counterfactual: without boundaries in the final decree and sale certificate, and with only the plot number stated, the result might have differed. The Court did not determine that hypothetical. Its actual conclusion was tied to the simultaneous presence of boundaries, the plot number, and the particular corroborating circumstances. References: IK-522414:L144-L145.
